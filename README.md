# Farmhouse Holdout

*Kessler Farm. Board up. Prepare for the horde. Survive as long as you can.*

Farmhouse Holdout is an isometric zombie survival roguelike that runs in your browser. You hold an old farmhouse
against wave after wave of the dead. Between waves you nail boards over the windows, craft ammo, upgrade your guns
and patch yourself up. When a wave is cleared you pick one card to make your run stronger. Play alone, or with up to
three friends online.

---

## Your survivor

**Character** on the title opens the character menu: your survivor stood in a little studio on the left, live and
big, that you can drag round and zoom in on, and on the right the parts of you as tabs along the top (the whole of
you, face, hair, hat, top, trousers, shoes, hands, gear) with everything each can be underneath, every change going
straight onto you while the camera glides in to the part you're changing. **Random** deals that part afresh (or the
whole of you), and **Keep this look every run** keeps the survivor you've made: it's who you are on the title, at the
start of every run and every time you're back on your feet in co-op, and it's remembered in your browser. With it off,
every run deals someone new. The cheat menu's character section is the same editor, with the same switch.

The studio lights you like a photo shoot: a warm key light high in front, a cool fill from the other side and two rim
lights from behind that pick your outline out of the dark, with a soft shadow on the floor under you. A bar over the
view turns you a quarter either way, shows you straight from the front or the back, sets the view back, and stops or
starts the slow turn. **Undo** and **Redo** sit by the close button (or Ctrl+Z, and Ctrl+Y or Ctrl+Shift+Z); each
change is a step, and dragging a slider is one step, not one per pixel. On the **Whole** tab every part's card has a
padlock: lock the parts you like and **Random** deals only the rest (a locked part shows a padlock on its tab too).
The tabs sit in one row that scrolls, the one you're on always brought into view, and ← / → (or Q / E) step through
them. The sliders fill up to their handle and the change follows as you drag; the menu scrolls with the game's own
scrollbar.

The clothes and marks are made to look like the real thing rather than stuck-on blocks: freckles, moles, bags,
lines and grime lie flat on the skin (a mole just raised), scars are a pale pink with a darker edge, and a scar
through an eyebrow leaves a gap in it. A work shirt or flannel has its button placket, buttons, and a chest pocket with
a buttoned flap (a flannel's plaid in pale lines); a tee its crew neck and a print flat on it; a hoodie the edge of its
hood, a kangaroo pocket with slanted openings, eyelets and thin drawcords with tipped ends; a polo its placket, buttons
and a small logo. Bedrolls come in real bedroll colours with a little variation, never the same twice.

Your pack is a proper hiking pack: a rounded body with a lid and a back flap, zips, rows of webbing with bar-tacked
stitching, side compression straps with ladder buckles, padded shoulder straps with a sternum strap and clip, a mesh
side pocket with a bottle in it and a zipped one on the other side, a pouch on the bottom and a grab loop on top, the
bedroll strapped under the lid with darker ends and cinch straps (holstered weapons still hang where they did). The
planks you carry (or metal bars, once you have the metalwork bench) are held on with cinch straps, the planks' end
grain and nail heads showing.
A watch or band sits right on your skin or sleeve, never floating off it. A chain, beads, strings or a metal watch
bracelet hang loose: drawn down by gravity whichever way your arm is turned, swinging on a spring as your arm moves
and turning a little round your wrist.

On your wrists you can wear a watch (field, sport, digital, leather, steel bracelet, and now and then gold or iced
out), a curb chain, beads, a rubber band, a stitched leather cuff with press studs, a cobra-weave paracord band or
braided strings, set for each wrist or the same on **Both**; the hands view shows both wrists. Each is built true to
size and round where the real thing is: a watch's round case with its bezel, markers, hour, minute and seconds hands,
lugs and crown; a chain's links each turned through the last; round beads. Your arms sit flush against your chest
(no gap at the shoulder), and the studio stands you with both arms the same length, however your hands were posed. You
blink now and then, in a run and in the studio, sometimes twice. When you're not busy your eyes look about: they rest
on a spot for a moment, then flick to the next (a quick dart that lands a hair past it and settles), off to one side
and back, a little up or down, often with a blink on a big look. They ease back to straight ahead while you aim, fire
or take a hit, wander a little less on the move, and do the same in the studio and on teammates. An eye patch's strap runs round the side of your head
from where it leaves your brow; the eye under it never blinks or squints, and with it over your aiming eye you aim with
the other one, that eye squinting along the gun and your head tipped over the stock the other way.

## How a run goes

1. **Break.** The clock is stopped. Board up the windows and doors, spend scrap at the stations and get into
   position. Press **T** when you're ready, or wait out the timer.
2. **Wave.** The zombies come out of the trees, break your boards down and climb in. Keep them out, and keep moving.
   From your own eyes they come from further off: deep in the haze out past the fields, solid all the way, a pale shape
   in the fog that picks up detail as it comes clear rather than appearing at the fence. They cover the hidden stretch
   quicker, so they reach the farm about as soon as they would seen from above. Your rounds reach out that far too (75 m
   from your own eyes, against 26 from above), so anything you can see out there you can shoot.
3. **Cards.** When the last zombie falls (time slows for a moment, the edges of the view cooling, with no black bars over it), pick one of three cards: a new gun, a skill, a perk or supplies. Every fifth
   wave brings a bonus hand. Or take a little scrap instead, a bit more each wave. You have 20 seconds to choose
   before one is taken for you; playing alone, that clock stands still while the game's tab is hidden or its window
   isn't in front, so stepping away doesn't choose for you (in co-op it runs on). The cards fit side by side in a small
   window too, everything tightened up on a short one. Whatever's still lying about glides
   in to you once the cards are done. Each kind of drop is modelled once, its overlapping faces pulled apart so nothing on it
   flickers, and copied for every one that falls, so a crowd dropping loot never stutters. Every drop lights the ground
   and the walls round it in its own colour (a crate's and a weapon's a little wider), more so after dark, dimming as it
   greys out when you're full and as it runs out.
   - Now and then a hand comes with a **sacrifice**. Give up three quarters of your scrap and three more cards are
     dealt (the rarer ones likelier), and you take two of the six.
   - Rarer, a **curse**: half your stamina, healing at a quarter strength with worse bleeding (Bad blood:
     double scrap), a quarter of your health gone for good (shown in hazard stripes on your bar), slower reloads, hits
     that hurt half again as much (Fragile), or half the scrap from kills, for the rest of the run. Some never come
     together. In return, three legendary cards join the hand (legendary perks, or tiered skills with every tier at
     once), and you take two. Taking a curse sounds like one: a low, sour slide down, not the reward chime.
   - **No-perk mode** (Mode, on the title screen under View) takes all of that away: no cards, no aid, no sacrifices or
     curses, and no slow motion as a wave clears; a second after the last zombie falls the next break starts. In co-op
     it's picked in the lobby beside the view, by the host, for everyone.
4. **Repeat.** Each wave is bigger and meaner than the last, and pays a little more scrap. The run ends when you go down.
   No two waves come at quite the same pace: each keeps to a rhythm of its own, the usual surges with a few seconds'
   lull between them, a trickle of small ones close together, or a few big rushes with long quiet between. The banner's
   line under the wave number changes from wave to wave too, and its sting climbs a semitone every three waves with a
   drum beat more every four, so the night gets tenser as it goes; a hard wave (a twist, the brutes, a fog round) has
   a harsher one.
   - From wave 4, about one wave in three comes with a **twist**, never two running and never on a fog round or a
     brute wave, its name over the banner in red: a **Stampede** (a quarter more of the wave sprinting, a touch weaker,
     in big rushes), a **Swarm** (half as many again, weaker, in one long stream that never lets up) or a **Siege**
     (fewer, but over half as tough again, in a trickle that doesn't stop), and from wave 6 a **Blood moon** (as the
     night comes on the moonlight, the sky, the haze and the dark all go red, and elites come four times as often).
     Clearing one pays a bounty: six scrap plus the wave's number, to everyone.
   - **Elites.** From wave 3, now and then one of the horde is something more (about 1 in 28 at first, climbing to 1
     in 11; on a blood moon up to 3 in 10): a walker, a sprinter or a brute (never a spitter), its eyes burning its own
     colour and seen through cover in that colour. Every elite has a brute's hide (it takes 40% less damage; an elite
     brute 55% less), and each kind is far more than the zombie under it:
     - **Hulking** (violet): five times the health, bigger, and its hits land 1.8 times as hard; a little slower.
     - **Frenzied** (red): half again as fast, clawing 1.8 times as often, with three times the health.
     - **Volatile** (green, from wave 5: swollen, glowing sores all over it): three times the health, and a beat after
       it dies it bursts into a cloud of red mist that hangs and drifts for a few seconds, blood thrown all round it.
       It doesn't wait to be killed, either: one that gets within 1.7 m of whoever it's after swells and shudders with
       a shriek for 0.65 s and goes off on its own (in co-op the host's game decides it). Within 3.8 m it hurts you (up
       to 35, less at the edge), leaves you reeling (the screen swimming, your aim going wide, slowed) and throws its
       blood over your view and over you and your gun. It shell-shocks you too: stunned on the spot for a second (no
       moving, no shooting; a little less at its edge), the world blurred and drained nearly grey and clearing over about
       three seconds, the blood on your view sharp over it, and your ears ringing, a high, thin, beating tone over the
       muffled world that fades as your hearing comes back; the zombies within 4.4 m aren't hurt, only thrown back and
       staggered (up to 1.6 s at its middle, a brute barely moved). A volatile brute's burst is a third bigger.

     A Hulking walker takes about 27 pistol body shots where a walker takes 4, a Hulking brute about 92. A wave's elites
     are settled as it starts, so a moment after the wave's own call (3.4 s in) one card says how many it's bringing,
     with each kind side by side: its icon in its colour, how many and its name. The cards are kept out of the way: small,
     in the top right corner, sliding in from the right, in the game's own notification style (a see-through dark panel
     with the elite's colour down its edge, its icon in a tinted tile, a soft sweep of light as it lands and a thin bar
     running out under it). One put down gets a card of its own on every player's
     screen (the skull, its line and what it was) and a moment's hit-stop. Each pays scrap (6 to 8, more each wave), in
     the pickups on the left: the whole of it to anyone who did half its damage or more, and to anyone who did less, that
     much of it as a share of the half (a quarter of the damage, half the scrap), in whole scrap. Whoever killed it also
     gets an ammo box for sure, and about half the time bandages or planks too. In co-op an elite is the same one on every screen.
   - **A stray crate.** From wave 2, about one wave in five, somewhere 25 to 50 seconds in, a chopper drops a crate
     outside the fence. Its card drops in under the top of the screen in the supply orange: a ring round the crate's
     icon running down the 15 seconds till it lands, the seconds big beside it, the side of the farm it's coming down on
     (north, north-east...). It doesn't say what's coming for it. After a few seconds it folds down to a slim strip (the
     ring, "Crate inbound", the side as N/NE..., the seconds, ticking over the last three) that stays till it lands, then
     turns green as "Crate down" for a few seconds or till you open it. The drop marker still counts down with how far
     off it is. While it's inbound, sprinters come for it (4, and one more every 3 waves, a second or two apart, sent by
     whoever runs the zombies in co-op), in through the fence's gaps on the side it's coming down on (one of the two
     gaps nearest it). They're part of the wave from the moment it's called: the zombies-left count and the wave bar
     take them in at once, and the wave isn't over till they've all come and been killed. A crate you call on the radio
     yourself brings none. It comes down out in the yard between the fence and the
     tree line, clear of every tree's crown, the bushes and rocks, and anything else stood there (the cars, the hay
     bales): a radio crate's ammo, 2 bandages, 4 planks, a cache of scrap (8, and 2 more a wave) and a military find.
     In co-op each player gets their own. The helicopter itself flies over, along its shadow's line at the height the
     crate falls from: an olive transport with its tail boom, fin and skids, a two-bladed rotor turning so fast it's
     mostly a blur, coming in over the trees and going, seen from your own eyes as well as from above, for the radio's
     drops too.

When the run ends the HUD clears away and the end screen comes up over the farm on its own; **Full run stats** folds open under your score: kills by zombie type and by weapon, headshots,
your longest kill and biggest chain, boards nailed and repaired, damage taken and your closest call (the lowest your health got that you lived through: a low only counts once you've
come through it and are still standing three seconds later, so the blow that finished you never does), scrap earned,
spent and sent to teammates, and every skill and perk you took.

## What's in it

**The farm.** Every run lays out a new house: its footprint, its rooms, its interior doors, its windows and where the
furniture stands, plus a new yard of cars, crates, hay bales and fences. The old rusted cars
have their hoods and trunks low below the windows: you see and shoot over those, and only the cabin is cover. The roof and walls fade away around you so
you can always see inside; from your own eyes the rooms have a proper ceiling over them instead, warm plaster with
stained timber joists running across the house, the lamps hanging from it on their cords. Indoors the roof keeps the
sun off everything under it, the floorboards' seams, the joists and everyone in the room (you, your own forearms from
your own eyes, and the dead) alike, so the rooms are lit by their lamps and the daylight through the windows, and out
in the yard you all go into the shade of the house and the trees. Furniture blocks sight, light and shots only where it really stands: a piece that
fills a little of its cell (the gun rack against the wall, a cabinet, a dresser) is measured to its own box, so the
part of the cell in front of it is open to see, light and shoot through, and the shadow it casts in the lighting is its
own shape, not the whole cell's, and you walk right up to it, bumping into the piece itself rather than the edge of its
cell. Only a piece filling most of its cell (85% or more each way) blocks the whole of it. The clock runs from afternoon into night, an hour every 30 seconds of a wave (so the sun and the moon drift across the sky rather than racing), and the dark is where the lamps, your
flashlight and the muzzle flashes matter. What the lamps don't reach isn't grey: the dark takes the cool of the sky,
a blue-violet moonlight by night and a cool blue-grey in the shade by day, and the shadows are graded cooler against the
warm of the light (with the lit colour a little fuller), so the honey daylight, the amber dusk and the lamplight have
something to stand against; by day the haze out over the woods is a cool blue-grey too, so the warm-lit farm stands
out from the distance. Each hour has its own grade (a lavender dawn, a violet dusk), eased between like the rest of
the light.

**Guns.** A 9mm handgun, a .357 revolver, a .50 hand cannon, pump, double-barrel and auto shotguns, a hunting rifle, a
battle rifle, a machine pistol, a submachine gun, an assault rifle and a light machine gun. Each one has its own
reload: mags in and out, shells one at a time, speed loaders and belts. The LMG's belt hangs off the gun as a real one
does: out of the feed tray flat, on out over its box and turning down in a short S, and straight down into the box near
its inner side, the rest of it folded away inside. The loop swings softly as the gun moves and nudges with each shot,
eased off toward the ends of its travel rather than snapping against them, while what's in the box stays put; the
rounds in the box go first as the belt runs down, then the loop from the box end. At the workbench you can add sights, lasers,
suppressors, stocks, grips and underbarrel grenades (a laser's beam is a fine line of light from its lens to its dot, the same width on screen however you look along it, never a flat strip), and change calibres with conversion kits such as AK mags for the
battle rifle (rocked in front lug first, the AK way) or 10mm for the SMG. The double barrel's sawn-off kit cuts it to a coach gun: more pellets in a wider spread, each hitting 30% harder up close, but its short barrels drop it off hard (full damage only to 2.5 m, down to a fifth of it by 7 m, where the full-length gun holds to 4 m and keeps half past 11). Down the sights it's quicker on its feet than the full-length gun too (76% of your pace against 66%). Every slug, out of any shotgun, holds its damage far further than shot but still tails off: full damage to 10 m, easing down to 60% by 26 m and never below that. Out of the sawn-off a slug drops off hard instead, as its buckshot does: full damage to 3 m and down to a fifth by 8 m. The double barrel itself was too strong early on: it's now eight pellets of 16 a barrel (from nine of 19: 256 for both barrels, from 342), its second barrel follows a touch slower (0.12 s) and each shell takes 0.5 s to load (from 0.42); still the biggest burst up close, not by so much. You can also load special rounds (hollow points, slugs, flechettes and more) and
tune damage, fire rate, recoil and magazine size. On the hunting rifle every optic goes on a picatinny rail along the
receiver, like the other guns: the red dot, the holo and the ACOG straight on it, the scopes in picatinny rings clamped
to it, both rings on the front of the receiver ahead of the ejection port, so nothing stands over the bolt (the barrel's
rear notch comes off for the rail). The hand cannon takes sights up to the ACOG: the red dot and the holo on its slide,
and a handgun scope or the ACOG on the rail along its barrel (which stays put while the slide works), held out at arm's
length with a handgun's long eye relief rather than up against your eye. An optic on the auto shotgun goes up on its rear
sight, as the real AA-12's does: a short picatinny rail across the top of the tall A-frame rear sight, running on
forward over the receiver, braced back down to it by a triangular steel plate with a triangle cut out of it. Both iron
sights stay up under it, so the optic sits high over the gun and the front post shows low in its window. A suppressor heats up the longer you keep firing through it, a little with each round, so it takes a
good many mags of sustained fire to get it hot (a full mag barely warms it), and it cools off slowly once you stop
(just for the look). A hotter powder warms it a little quicker: hand-loads about 8% more a round, +P loads 16%,
match-grade 24%. Warm, the
barrel smokes, thin wisps curling up off the muzzle with a puff at each shot; hotter, the whole can, its bands and
clamp too, glows a dull red, then orange, then a deep yellow at its hottest (it never washes out to white), with a slight
yellow bloom round it; and overheating, the smoke pours
off it slow and heavy and hangs in the air, so moving or swinging the gun leaves a trail of it behind. Left alone it
fades back down through the same colours, still smoking a while. However fast you fire, the smoke keeps coming: with a lot of smoke and
dust in the air at once, it's the faintest, nearly faded puff that's reused for a new one, so nothing that's still
plainly there ever blinks out.

**Melee.** An ash bat, crowbar, pipe wrench, machete, katana, fire axe and sledgehammer, each with its own swings,
reach and stamina cost. Sharp blades take heads and limbs off. The bat is one smooth turned piece of ash; its Nails are real
nails driven right through the barrel, round heads on the wood and points out the far side, and Barbed wire, its own
mod now (no nails needed), is two twisted strands wound up the barrel with barbs every few centimetres. The crowbar is
one red hex bar, the steel worn bright at both ends: a short angled pry foot with a nail slot, the long shaft and a
tight gooseneck running straight into a flattened claw split into two prongs, with no gap where the bar meets it. The katana's curved blade is one piece, its spine, hamon and edge running unbroken to the point, and it's held by
its wrapped handle, both hands on the grip below the guard. From your own eyes a melee weapon in your hands keeps your
body together with your legs, and a swing doesn't carry you forward. From your own eyes each swing is kept where you
can see it: the wind-up stays at the top of the view rather than behind your head, your arm never sweeps through your
face, and the blow crosses the view. The weapon is swung round by your arm and held in the fist as it is in the guard,
so it never spins about its handle or flips over in your hand, and the forearm's roll and the weapon's tilt ease in
and out of every turn instead of starting and stopping dead. Through a swing the edge (the axe's bit, a blade's edge)
faces round the way the swing turns: leading the blow, trailing as it's drawn back, still leading as it carries on
past and comes back round, so the axe's head stays upright through an overhead chop all the way down, from your own
eyes and from above, never turned flat on its side at the bottom or flipped over about the handle.

**Every kill lands.** A round or a blow of yours that hits flashes a marker on your crosshair: four short strokes on
the diagonals, white, gold through the head, and a bigger red one for a kill. A hit down the sights in first person
(where the crosshair's put away) marks nothing, so no marker turns up when you come off the sights, however soon. A kill also ticks, a short bright note
that climbs a semitone with each kill in a chain, and shakes the view by how hard it landed: a light round a nudge, a
heavy round, a shotgun or a heavy blow much more, through the head or with a big overkill more again. Playing alone, a
kill that lands hard (through the head with anything but a fast automatic, a heavy round, a shotgun, a heavy blow)
holds the world still for a few hundredths of a second, never closer together than a third of a second, so a run of
kills doesn't stutter. Kills in a chain (each within 1.6 seconds of the last) are called out on a small card above the
middle, Double kill to Unstoppable: a pip for each kill in the chain, the scrap it's paid, and a thin bar draining over
the time left to keep it going. Each kill pops it in again with its newest pip bursting in, its glow hotter the longer
the chain runs, and it fades as soon as the chain can't go on. A long chain pays a little scrap as it grows: one at its 3rd, 5th and 7th kills, then one every 5 kills from the
10th (a chain of 10 pays 4, of 20 pays 6), not a scrap for every kill in it.
A kill sounds like what made it: through the head the skull bursting (two recordings), through the body it giving
way with a deep thud as it drops (four), heavier for a blade or a club, a hiss for a burning one; most of the time
with the zombie's last breath going out of it. Bullets into flesh have four recordings rather than two. The new
recordings are from the pack already in use (`assets/sounds/Guns and stuffs.zip`, cut by `tools/make-sounds.mjs`).

**Their voices.** Every zombie has a voice of its own, pitched for it when it turns up: a walker anywhere from a low
moan to a reedy one, a sprinter's a ragged shriek that climbs (and it lets one out as it comes out of the treeline,
most of the time), a brute's a deep, rasping roar (as it charges, too), a spitter's a wet gurgle. They're heard from
where they are, quieter and off to the side with distance and muffled through the walls, and it's now one, now
another of the few nearest you, more often the more of them are close by, so a crowd round the house moans over
itself.

**Fog rounds.** Once in a while (never within three waves of the last) a wave comes in under a thick ground fog that rolls in off the fields. It's a real bank
of fog with height: densest low down and thinning up to a billowing top that rises and falls across the yard, with
thicker and thinner patches through it, paler at its tops and darker in its depths. From above, taller things (a tree,
the burn barrel, a fence post) stand up out of it and its billows shift as the view moves; from your own eyes it's a
wall of mist at waist and head height with the tree tops and the sky showing over it, brightening looking toward the
sun. It stays out of the house, parts round you in a ring that opens a little wider higher up, and anything out in it
can't be seen, outlined or picked out by your crosshair until it's nearly on you. Lamps and the fire glow in it, and
your flashlight's beam lights it up and cuts a clear path through it. Smoke, sparks and the like near you aren't
buried under the fog beyond them: each shows as much as it would standing where it is (clear in the open ring round
you, gone deep out in the fog), and a sight's reticle always shows through it. The fog is worked out at a third of the
screen's resolution (a ninth of the pixels, its dearest part by far; from above, where a look crosses only its shallow
layer, with fewer steps through it too) and brought up to full size by depth, so the edge of anything standing in front
of it stays clean. Down a scope it's there just as it is round it, worked out for the scope's own view. A zombie only
faintly there doesn't hide the fog behind it, so one all but lost in the fog never shows as a clear patch in its shape.

**Zombies.** Shambling walkers, fast runners, spitters that lob acid at anyone who stands still (the splat it leaves is
nearly four metres across and lasts fourteen seconds: a bubbling green pool, bubbles swelling all over it and popping
in bright rings under an oily swirl, glowing on its own at night; standing in it burns for 2 every half second, rising
to 6 after four seconds in it, and drags at your feet), and brutes that
charge through fences and plough through the crowd: from 4 to 11 m off with a clear line to you, a brute crouches and
charges more often than not within a second or two (and again 6 to 10 s after), a red lane on the ground showing where. Every zombie it ploughs into is knocked off its feet (one square in its path as the brute reaches it, one it clips thrown off to the side it was on), lies there a moment and gets back up; only a crawler is just shoved along the ground. (They used to stay frozen where they fell: two files had a function of the same name, and in the one script the player's gun-kick spring had replaced the zombies' knockdown step. The build now fails on any two top-level functions with the same name.) Their hits are gentle on the first nights: on wave 1 a zombie hits
for 55% of its full damage (a walker for about 16 out of your 100 health, not 29), rising evenly to full by wave 8,
and from there each wave hits 3% harder as before. You walk right up to the fence: it stops you at its rails and posts, not a stride short of them. Shots land where they hit: a leg shot slows them, a severed leg
leaves a crawler (lying low along the ground, its hips and what's left of its legs dragging, its chest propped on its
forearms), and a headshot drops them. A zombie that's lost a limb bleeds out from the stump, slowly: 2.5 health a
second for a whole arm or leg, 1.5 for a forearm or shin, up to 7.5 however many it's lost, dripping blood as it goes
and with no damage numbers. Blood on the ground stains the ground and what stands fixed on it, never you or a teammate standing in it. A shotgun's load (buckshot, a slug or flechettes; not
dragon's breath) blows a limb to red mist when enough of one blast lands on it: three pellets on an arm or a leg (four
flechettes, or a single slug), however much of it was left, gone where it was in a cloud of red with scraps of it
tumbling away. A kill through the head with any of them blows the head to mist every time from up close, and further
off when two or more pellets went into it. Close by (within about 3 m), a limb or a head coming off or blown apart,
your doing or a teammate's, spatters your view with blood, as if on your face, thrown from the side it happened on: a
faint red haze from that edge, a spray of fine specks, wobbly-edged drops (deep red, darker where it pools at the rim,
thinner in the middle, a soft sheen and a small glint) and, from a close one, a splat or two with spikes and beads flung
out of them, all soft and out of focus. The bigger drops run down leaving a thin trail, and it fades in a couple of
seconds. It gets on you and what's in your hands as well (the same stain as from fighting; each splash of blood on you
or a weapon stays 30 seconds and then fades out over 3, and a cleared wave cleans you up anyway): a limb blown to mist
throws about twice what a clean cut does, and a volatile going off near you more than either. It's the same few a second on any night, so on later nights, with more health, they take
longer to bleed out. Dragon's breath sets a zombie alight where its pellets hit (not where they hit armour: a helmet with its face shield

the last, a last turn straight round, and the end pressed down and taped. A short bandage gets fewer turns. The
forearm turns slowly in the hand as the gauze goes round, and the hand holding it drifts a little with your breathing,
never on a straight line: the arm comes up quickly and settles with a touch of overshoot, each turn of the roll
slows where it pulls the strip tight and quickens round the back, and the arm sways on a couple of slow, uneven waves. From your own eyes the view goes with the work rather than staring fixed: it tips down onto the arm and turns a touch toward it, tilted over to it, nods each time the strip's pulled tight over the top of a turn, sways after the hand as it goes round, rises and falls with a slow breath, and follows the wrap up the forearm as it goes on; all of it eased onto softly as the arms come up and settling back more slowly once the bandage is done.
In first person the forearm is held close in front of you, slanting across the middle of the view, and the view tips down a little
onto it, so the wrap goes on where you can watch it, and the right hand comes in round it with the roll (its shoulder
placed so the hand stays at the arm, the strip off the roll only a few centimetres long); it all stays where it is in the view as you look up or down, as the weapons do. Your teammates see your arms,
bandaging or with a melee weapon, turned where you're looking up or down, as they already did with a gun. Each bandage stays on your arm
till the wave's over (cleared, cards or no cards, they come off with the blood on you and your weapons), up to three of them up the forearm, wound snug over whatever's there: the bare arm, a sleeve, a
glove, the canvas jacket's sleeve, a rolled-up sleeve, a watch. Each one follows what's under it along the arm (tight on
a sleeve, up over a cuff's edge or a roll and straight back down past it, drawn up over a snap or a watch face the way
a bandage pulled tight is, never floating off the arm and never with anything poking through it), its corners pulled
tight round the arm's, and each turn sits only on the turns actually under it, a gauze layer's thickness over each, with a
spot of blood slowly seeping through each. The blood you pick up killing zombies up close stains your clothes and
skin, never a fresh bandage: those stay clean but for their own seep.

**Reloads that go wrong.** Hurt hands reload slower and shakier. They can fumble: the hands lose their rhythm,
slowing to about half speed and catching it back up (never stopping, which read as the game hitching), while the hand
with the mag juggles it, a dip, a catch and a wobble, the gun knocked partway through and something clacking. The revolver never fumbles: its rounds go in one at a time anyway. They can also miss the magwell, in one continuous motion: the fresh mag drifts off the well's line as it comes up,
knocks into its lip with a short clack (the gun jolted by it), bounces back off it as the bounce dies away, and is eased back onto the line and in. No two misses look alike: each is its own size (most small), its own bounce, and one after another come from alternate sides. That
can happen once, twice or up to three times running at low health, and once in a while with steady hands too. The mag
is only ever off the line below the well's mouth, so it never goes through the side of the gun. A hand leaving the gun
for a mag, a shell or a handle gathers speed rather than starting off at full tilt, and when a reload's done the hand
carries on back to its grip with the way it was going, settling onto it without stopping short or overshooting. Every one of these beats
is part of the reload's own length, so the reload ring fills over the whole reload as it really goes (a fumble or a
miss isn't known ahead: the ring fills as the reload's planned to go, and only stretches out once the hands actually
slip), and the old mag falling, the new one going home and their sounds all land on the animation's own marks. The
ring round the crosshair glides after the reload rather than stepping, and when a fumble or a miss sets it back it
slides back down with a flair: it flares red, the bead on its end swells and the ring gives a short shake. The shake
moves the ring on top of where it's drawn round the crosshair, so it stays in its place (it used to be thrown down
across the crosshair and stay there).

**A gun in each hand.** With the second pistol or second machine pistol fitted, left click fires the gun on your left and
right click the one on your right, and each gun kicks on its own shot from
your own eyes, flipping up on its wrist while the other stays steady, and running with them each hand pumps forward
and back with your stride, opposite the other, the guns canted out. Inspecting a pair (**Y**) has its own moves: each
gun is turned on its own wrist rather than the two swung about as one, the left a beat behind the right and mirroring
it. Both come up and in, roll onto their sides to show their outsides, roll right over to show their insides, stand
muzzles up and toed in so you see both muzzles side by side, then tip down so you look along the slides, and go back
onto the aim. From your own eyes they're brought up into view, held out where the fists don't hide them. A shot from
either gun (left or right click) ends it at once.

**Inspecting.** Press **Y** (it can be rebound) to look your gun over, from your own eyes or from above: it's brought
up and in, rolled over to show one side and then the other, swung round to see down its front, and tipped to look
along its back and top, your head following it. Each move starts gently, swings through and settles into its pose
with a little give, the hands never quite still. The hand on the grip keeps hold of it as it was however the gun's
turned (the wrist turns with it), and the gun's never held further out or nearer in than your arms comfortably reach,
so it never floats off the hand or turns in it. Standing close to a wall doesn't spoil it: the gun's no longer pushed
back and dipped away from the wall while you look it over (it's brought in close anyway), easing back into the wall's
push as you finish. A handgun's turned in the one hand, the other hanging at your side.
From your own eyes the gun's held up in front of you and the HUD and the crosshair are put away while you look (from
above they stay). Every sidearm but the revolver (the handgun and the .50 alike) is looked over the same way: brought
up close in the middle of your view and turned on its side in the hand, its muzzle tipped up to the left and its top
to the right, your thumb along the frame, then rolled right over to its other side, stood muzzle up and tipped down to
look along the slide. The revolver's twirled first: held out to the side on a loose wrist, a little wind back, then
flicked forward round your trigger finger for three turns, quick off the hand and slowing all the way into the palm
(never at a steady rate), caught with a small rock past the grip, and then looked over just like the handgun. With a
shoulder stock on it there's no twirling it: it's only looked over. Every long gun has its own way of being looked over, after what you'd check
on that one: the pump's rolled onto its side to look into the loading port, then
its ejection port, the bead and the ghost ring; the double's muzzles are tipped right up to look over the rib at both
bores before each side plate (the sawn-off, short and light, is handled on the wrist instead: tipped muzzles up close to the eye to look down both bores, rolled right over to each side plate, quicker, and tipped down along its stub of a rib); the auto shotgun shows its left side and its mag from below; the hunting rifle is
sighted along its top first, unhurried, then rolled to its bolt; the battle rifle's canted forty-five degrees each
way and lifted to look at its mag well; the machine pistol's light, so it's flicked about quickly; the SMG shows its
cocking tube, the front sight's hood and the drum sight; the AR its left side, the port and forward assist, and its
mag well; the LMG's heavy, so it's only hefted and tipped a little each way before a look over the feed cover. At the
end it's carried back onto the aim as smoothly as it was turned. Pausing or picking cards between waves doesn't cut it short or start it over: it comes to a stop right where it is under the menu, frozen mid-move from your own eyes and from above alike (the hands' small shake stops with it), and carries on from there once the menu's gone. A melee weapon's looked over its own way: a one-hander (the
crowbar, the wrench, the machete) is brought up in front of your face, turned to show one flat and then the other,
laid across to look along its edge and twirled once in the fingers before it drops back to the guard; the katana's
held across in both hands at the chest and turned, then stood up to look up its edge; the axe, the sledge and the bat
are hefted up in front of you, their head turned each way, then tipped out to your right and turned there. Each one's
carried from pose to pose on the same springs as its swings, slower for a heavy one. Firing or reloading puts the gun straight
back on the aim for it; aiming, sprinting, vaulting, switching weapons or pressing Y again eases it back.

## Working on the game

```bash
npm install
npm run dev        # builds and serves at http://localhost:5173, rebuilding on every save
npm run build      # minified build into dist/
npm run build:dev  # readable build with a source map, so errors point at src/js/<file>:<line>
npm run fix-imports # rewrites every file's imports and exports to match what it uses (after moving code about)
```

Every build also writes `dist/standalone.html`, the whole game in one file. Every push runs CI: all three builds and a
browser smoke test (`tools/smoke.mjs`) that starts a run, moves, shoots, reloads and pauses.

**Keeping a crowd cheap to draw.** A zombie you can see is about a dozen draws for its body and the same again for its
shadow, plus three more per body piece for the see-through outline that shows it behind cover from above. Those
see-through layers are only drawn when something could actually stand between the camera and the zombie: a march from
the zombie toward the camera across the farm's grid (walls, window frames, fences, props up to their height, trees'
crowns, the roof over the house, the woods past the edge) decides it, leaning the safe way. Its eyes, too small to
change its shape, get none. A gun rebuilt mid-run (an attachment, mod or upgrade bought at the bench) is merged back
into a mesh per material once its pieces settle, like one built at the start of a run, rather than staying a hundred
separate draws. With twelve zombies out in the yard at night that's about 930 draws a frame instead of about 1,290,
and with a weapon light and laser on the gun about 900 instead of about 1,470.
Every figure (you, the zombies, your teammates) also leaves out its pieces too small to see from where the camera is:
a piece under about a pixel across on screen (an eyelid, a buckle, a zip, a stowed gun's pin) is moved onto a layer
nothing draws, out of the frame, its shadow and its outline, and put back as the camera comes close enough for it to
show (first person, down the sights). Your pieces and your teammates' a little bigger than that, under four pixels,
stay in the frame but stop casting a shadow nobody could make out. Your own see-through outline (a fill, a silhouette
and a rim for each piece of you, some hundred and seventy draws) and your teammates' are only drawn when something
could stand between the camera and you, the same test as the zombies'. With 24 zombies round the house that's about
1,010 draws a frame instead of about 1,190, and in the yard about 1,060 instead of about 1,270.
Every gun also has a far version that looks the same but is made of far fewer triangles, drawn instead of the
detailed one once the whole gun is under about 150 pixels long on screen: the guns in the rack, on your back, in
your and your teammates' hands from above, a teammate's across the yard in first person. Each switches by its own size
on screen, for whichever camera is drawing it, with a little give either side so it doesn't flicker at the edge. The
gun in your hands in first person, or one you walk right up to, stays fully detailed. The far version keeps every
part's outline. Stocks are bent along the gun to within 0.8 mm instead of 0.1 mm. Barrels, rings and lathe-turned
parts get half the sides, and outlines keep only the points needed to stay within half a millimetre. Of the stippling and checkering, only the
faces you can see are kept (a dot's or a line's top, not its sides), so the grip texture still shows. A spitter's acid sacs are rounded with fewer faces once the spitter's under about 140 pixels tall.
In the house with the gun rack in view, that's about 211,000 triangles a frame instead of about 398,000.
The woods only draw the trees you can see. Each frame, trees out of view are left out of the frame, and from your
own eyes so are trees hidden behind nearer ones. That's checked against a small depth picture of the view: nearest
first, each tree that shows paints in boxes that sit inside its crown (a staircase up each tier of a pine, the middle
of a broadleaf's crown), and a tree whose whole outline is behind them is left out. The boxes never reach past the
boughs, so a tree you'd see through a gap is always drawn. Same-frame renders with and without it come out identical.
From your own eyes that takes the woods from 2,475 trees to about 300–650 drawn, depending on where you look. A tree
left out of the frame still casts its shadow, from a copy drawn only into the shadow map.
Everything else that stands still is culled the same way. From your own eyes (and the free camera) the house's outer
walls go into that depth picture first, as the faces of each run of wall cells. Window and door cells are left out, so
whatever you'd see through an opening is always drawn. So the woods behind the house are left out too. Then every
piece of the house, the furniture and the yard's props that's wholly behind a wall or a tree crown is left out of the
frame. From any view, a piece under about half a pixel across on screen (a hinge, a nail, a screw far off) is also left
out. Its size on screen comes from the camera's own field of view, so with a wide one small things far off go sooner,
as they look smaller. Pieces behind you or off to the side were already left out, against the same camera. Only the
frame is culled, never the shadow map (the sun sees what you can't) or a scope's view. Same-frame renders with and
without it came out identical. Facing the house from outside, a frame went from about 224,000 triangles and 600 draws to
137,000 and 390; from the side, 218,000 and 600 to 173,000 and 430; inside, about 15% fewer triangles.
The frame's own work was trimmed too. A profile of a wave in first person showed the game's code is a small share of
the frame and the drawing nearly all of it, so draws were what to cut. Past the culling above, the glow and the fog's
overlay passes no longer work out every object's place in the world over again, just after the frame itself did. Reading
where the muzzle or your eye is (asked for several times a frame) updates only the chain of parts it hangs from, not your
whole body; checked the same to the last digit. A piece of you or a teammate under 4 cm across casts no shadow, as the
sun's shadows are softened over more than that. That's 70 shadow draws of you a frame down to 62.
A kill used to stall the frame for 12 to 20 ms of the game's own code on its own, and it's what made the later waves,
with kills coming thick and fast, judder: the HUD replayed its little animations (the kill and scrap counters' pop,
the crosshair's hit marker, the streak and pickup notes, a damage number's bump on every hit) by asking the page where
an element was in between, which makes the browser lay the whole page out there and then, several times a kill. They're
replayed a frame later instead, without asking, and the counters' rolling digits the same way: a kill is now about
1.3 ms.
The trees outside the fence now cast shadows too. Those within reach of the sun's shadows cast real ones, and the far
woods (past about 32 m from the middle, beyond the shadow map) lay a soft shadow on the ground away from the sun,
as long as the tree's height over the sun's angle, turned with the sun through the day and fading with it. Every tree
outside the farm also has a soft pool of shade under it, as the farm's own do.
Every model was also put through a triangle pass that keeps every detail and only cuts triangles that don't need to be
there:
- **Stocks** (the hunting rifle's, the pump's, the double barrel's, the MP5's) are slimmed along the gun by cutting
  them into pieces. They used to be cut every 8 mm all over, so their big flat sides were tens of thousands of slivers.
  Now an edge is only cut where the slimming really bends it, until it's within a tenth of a millimetre of the curve.
  Flat stretches stay a few big triangles, and the wrist's curve gets as many as it needs.
- **Outlines:** every traced part drops the points it can do without (on a straight run, or more than a curve needs to
  stay within 0.04 mm), so its sides and faces have fewer triangles and an identical shape.
- **Stippling and checkering:** each dot keeps its flat top, with each side a single triangle down to the grip, and
  never its hidden face against the grip (6 triangles instead of 12). Each checkering line keeps its top and two
  sides, but not its hidden underside or its two ends, a millimetre square each (6 instead of 12). At a couple of
  pixels across they look the same.
- **Trees:** the trunk's hidden ends (one in the ground, one up inside the boughs) are gone. Every flat end is fanned
  from a corner, so an 8-sided cone's base is 6 triangles, not 8. The grass tufts' bases are fanned the same way. A
  tree gets lighter the less of it you can see. From your own eyes, one under 64 pixels tall, or with over 40% of it
  behind nearer trees, is drawn with 6-sided tiers on a 5-sided trunk. One under 26 pixels, or over 75% hidden, gets
  5-sided tiers on a 4-sided trunk. One wholly hidden isn't drawn at all. Same-frame renders with and without the
  lighter trees differ in about one pixel in a thousand. The trees' shadows are cast from that lighter version too,
  as the sun's shadows are softened over more than the two ever differ by.
- **The bat's** turned outline keeps only the points its curves need (a tenth of a millimetre), not 90 evenly spread.
- **Wrist pieces:** the beads, knots and chain links are only a few millimetres across, so they're rounded with fewer
  sides (a paracord band was about 23,000 triangles).
- **Tiny parts' shadows:** the house's, the furniture's and the props' shadows leave out parts under 3 cm, whose
  shadows the soft shadow edge would hide anyway.

### How the code fits together

The game is plain JavaScript and three.js (r186, WebGL2), with no framework. The build bundles just the parts of
three.js the game uses into `vendor/three.min.js`. Each file in `src/js` is an ES module:

- A file imports what it uses from the other files and exports what they use from it, so the top of a file lists
  everything it depends on, and an editor can jump to where anything is defined.
- Another file's variable can only be read. The few that other files change come with a setter in their own file
  (`setShake`, `setZoom` and so on).
- The build checks every file's imports and exports (`tools/modules.mjs`). It fails, naming the file and line, on:
  - a name that isn't imported;
  - an import of something that isn't exported, or an import that isn't used;
  - an export no other file imports, or anything declared and never used;
  - an assignment to another file's variable;
  - a file using, as it loads, something from a file that loads after it.
- To move code from one file to another, move it and run `npm run fix-imports`: it rewrites the imports and exports
  of every file to match.
- For the browser, the build joins the files in the order of `src/js/load-order.json` into one script. That order only
  matters for code that runs at load time, such as building the world. The build also checks the joined script and
  names the file and line of any error, such as two files declaring the same name.
- Two pieces of one model whose faces lie in the same plane, facing the same way, flicker through each other where they
  overlap (z-fighting). The model helpers (`box` in `core/geometry.js`, `profile`, `solid` and `cyl` in
  `weapons/gun-parts.js`) note each face's plane and the patch it covers, per group; a face that would lie over one
  already there is pushed out a hair (0.1 mm on gun-sized parts, 0.6 mm on anything over 25 cm), so the later piece (the
  detail on a body, as models are built) always shows. Pieces side by side in a row aren't touched, and nothing is
  pushed more than three times. Build models body first, details after, and this takes care of itself.
- Each frame, `game/loop.js` runs `update(dt)` (`game/update.js`, the simulation) and then `visuals(dt)`
  (`game/visuals.js`, animation, camera and HUD). Those two files list every system in order, so they're the place to
  start reading.

```
src/
  index.html     the page: HUD, menus, settings, station panels
  styles/        CSS, one file per part of the screen
  js/
    core/        renderer, shaders, maths, geometry helpers, shadow batching
    world/       the farm: house, rooms, yard, props, collision, cut-away
    lighting/    light map, day and night, fog of war
    player/      movement, aiming, vaulting, bandages, gear
    weapons/     gun stats, models, mods, firing, reloading
    melee/       melee weapons and swings
    zombies/     types, spawning, pathfinding, damage, ragdolls
    barricades/  boards and grates
    stations/    workbench, sawhorse, cabinet, radio, gun rack, strongbox
    upgrades/    the cards and tiers
    effects/     post-processing (SSAO, bloom), blood, particles, outlines
    ui/          HUD, crosshair, settings, menus
    input/       keyboard, mouse, touch and key bindings
    audio/       sounds and reverb
    game/        shared state, the loop, the camera
    net/         co-op over WebRTC
tools/           build, smoke test, and the asset packing scripts
assets/          the raw texture and sound packs (not shipped)
```

Each source file starts with a comment saying what it holds. The main shared state is `P` (the player), `W8` (your
weapons and ammo), `S` (your upgrade stats), `run` (the current run), `zombies`, and `state` and `phase` (which screen
is showing, and whether it's a wave or a break).

### Adding a gun

1. Add its stats to `GUN_WEAPONS` in `weapons/gun-stats.js`. Give it a rarity (`rar`) and a `desc`, and it will turn
   up as a card.
2. Add a model builder with the same id to `GUN_MODELS` in `weapons/gun-models.js`. Copying the closest gun is the
   easiest start.
3. Optionally add mods for it in `weapons/gun-mods.js`, a range drop-off in `weapons/ammo.js` and an ejection style in
   `weapons/casings.js`.

Melee weapons work the same way, in `melee/melee-stats.js` and `melee/melee-models.js`.
