# Farmhouse Holdout

*Kessler Farm. Board up. Prepare for the horde. Survive as long as you can.*

Farmhouse Holdout is an isometric zombie survival roguelike that runs in your browser. You hold an old farmhouse
against wave after wave of the dead. Between waves you nail boards over the windows, craft ammo, upgrade your guns
and patch yourself up. When a wave is cleared you pick one card to make your run stronger. Play alone, or with up to
three friends online.

---

## Your survivor

**Character** on the title opens the character menu: your survivor stood in a little studio on the left, live and
big, that you can drag round and zoom in on, and on the right the parts of you as tabs along the top (the whole of
you, face, hair, hat, top, trousers, shoes, hands, gear) with everything each can be underneath, every change going
straight onto you while the camera glides in to the part you're changing. **Random** deals that part afresh (or the
whole of you), and **Keep this look every run** keeps the survivor you've made: it's who you are on the title, at the
start of every run and every time you're back on your feet in co-op, and it's remembered in your browser. With it off,
every run deals someone new. The cheat menu's character section is the same editor, with the same switch.

The studio lights you like a photo shoot: a warm key light high in front, a cool fill from the other side and two rim
lights from behind that pick your outline out of the dark, with a soft shadow on the floor under you. A bar over the
view turns you a quarter either way, shows you straight from the front or the back, sets the view back, and stops or
starts the slow turn. **Undo** and **Redo** sit by the close button (or Ctrl+Z, and Ctrl+Y or Ctrl+Shift+Z); each
change is a step, and dragging a slider is one step, not one per pixel. On the **Whole** tab every part's card has a
padlock: lock the parts you like and **Random** deals only the rest (a locked part shows a padlock on its tab too).
The tabs sit in one row that scrolls, the one you're on always brought into view, and ← / → (or Q / E) step through
them. The sliders fill up to their handle and the change follows as you drag; the menu scrolls with the game's own
scrollbar.

**Survivor slots.** A row of chips over the tabs holds up to eight saved survivors to swap between: **+ New** (or **+
Save this survivor** when there are none) saves the look you have on as a new slot; clicking a chip puts that survivor
on (Undo takes it back); the slot you're wearing has its name to type over, only a label for telling them apart, not
your name in co-op; a dot on it turns orange once you've changed the look since saving, and **Save** puts the changes
into it; the cross deletes it (a second click to be sure). A look you'd kept before becomes Survivor 1. They're
remembered in your browser.

**Scrolling the tabs.** The row of parts along the top (and the slots row) scrolls: the mouse wheel moves it sideways,
you can drag it (a drag never clicks a tab), and there's a thin scrollbar.

**In the lobby.** Your own seat in a co-op or arena lobby has a **Change look** button that opens the customizer over
the lobby; the others see your new look once you click Done (or close it any other way), not at every change.

**The preview** leaves off the planks (and metal bars) strapped to your pack, and the bat lies slanted across the
back of the pack against its canvas, an automatic weapon over it, rather than hanging off its side in the air. In a
run they're carried as before.

**Beards.** A beard that goes round the face (a full beard, a long beard, a chinstrap, mutton chops) wraps round it: its front reaches out over the corners of the head, its sides run back from there to the ears, and a full beard or a chinstrap carries on under the jaw, so from the side or from below there's no bare corner and no edge standing off in the air (the front used to stop short of the corners and the sides short of the front). The mouth sits on your face with every beard; it's only lifted onto the beard where a beard covers it
(a full beard, a long beard), so a goatee, a Van Dyke, a soul patch, a horseshoe, mutton chops or a moustache no
longer leaves it floating out in front of you. **Stubble** and **heavy stubble** are stubble: a flat shadow in the
skin over the jaw, chin, upper lip, the sides of the face and under the chin, with a paler edge and a scatter of fine
dark bristles (the same on every screen), heavy stubble darker and denser, rather than a raised block of beard.

The clothes and marks are made to look like the real thing rather than stuck-on blocks: freckles, moles, bags,
lines and grime lie flat on the skin (a mole just raised), scars are a pale pink with a darker edge, and a scar
through an eyebrow leaves a gap in it. A work shirt or flannel has its button placket, buttons, and a chest pocket with
a buttoned flap (a flannel's plaid in pale lines); a tee its crew neck and a print flat on it; a hoodie the edge of its
hood, a kangaroo pocket with slanted openings, eyelets and thin drawcords with tipped ends; a polo its placket, buttons
and a small logo. Bedrolls come in real bedroll colours with a little variation, never the same twice.

Your pack is a proper hiking pack: a rounded body with a lid and a back flap, zips, rows of webbing with bar-tacked
stitching, side compression straps with ladder buckles, padded shoulder straps with a sternum strap and clip, a mesh
side pocket with a bottle in it and a zipped one on the other side, a pouch on the bottom and a grab loop on top, the
bedroll strapped under the lid with darker ends and cinch straps (holstered weapons still hang where they did). The
planks you carry (or metal bars, once you have the metalwork bench) are held on with cinch straps, the planks' end
grain and nail heads showing.
A watch or band sits right on your skin or sleeve, never floating off it. A chain, beads, strings or a metal watch
bracelet hang loose: drawn down by gravity whichever way your arm is turned, swinging on a spring as your arm moves
and turning a little round your wrist.

On your wrists you can wear a watch (field, sport, digital, leather, steel bracelet, and now and then gold or iced
out), a curb chain, beads, a rubber band, a stitched leather cuff with press studs, a cobra-weave paracord band or
braided strings, set for each wrist or the same on **Both**; the hands view shows both wrists. Each is built true to
size and round where the real thing is: a watch's round case with its bezel, markers, hour, minute and seconds hands,
lugs and crown; a chain's links each turned through the last; round beads. Your arms sit flush against your chest
(no gap at the shoulder), and the studio stands you with both arms the same length, however your hands were posed. You
blink now and then, in a run and in the studio, sometimes twice. When you're not busy your eyes look about: they rest
on a spot for a moment, then flick to the next (a quick dart that lands a hair past it and settles), off to one side
and back, a little up or down, often with a blink on a big look. They ease back to straight ahead while you aim, fire
or take a hit, wander a little less on the move, and do the same in the studio and on teammates. An eye patch's strap runs round the side of your head
from where it leaves your brow; the eye under it never blinks or squints, and with it over your aiming eye you aim with
the other one, that eye squinting along the gun and your head tipped over the stock the other way.

## How a run goes

1. **Break.** The clock is stopped. Board up the windows and doors, spend scrap at the stations and get into
   position. Press **T** when you're ready, or wait out the timer.
2. **Wave.** The zombies come out of the trees, break your boards down and climb in. Keep them out, and keep moving.
   From your own eyes they come from further off: deep in the haze out past the fields, solid all the way, a pale shape
   in the fog that picks up detail as it comes clear rather than appearing at the fence. They cover the hidden stretch
   quicker, so they reach the farm about as soon as they would seen from above. Your rounds reach out that far too (75 m
   from your own eyes, against 26 from above), so anything you can see out there you can shoot.
3. **Cards.** When the last zombie falls (time slows for a moment, the edges of the view cooling, with no black bars over it), pick one of three cards: a new gun, a skill, a perk or supplies. Every fifth
   wave brings a bonus hand. Or take a little scrap instead, a bit more each wave. You have 20 seconds to choose
   before one is taken for you; playing alone, that clock stands still while the game's tab is hidden or its window
   isn't in front, so stepping away doesn't choose for you (in co-op it runs on). The cards fit side by side in a small
   window too, everything tightened up on a short one. Whatever's still lying about glides
   in to you once the cards are done. Each kind of drop is modelled once, its overlapping faces pulled apart so nothing on it
   flickers, and copied for every one that falls, so a crowd dropping loot never stutters. Every drop lights the ground
   and the walls round it in its own colour (a crate's and a weapon's a little wider), more so after dark, dimming as it
   greys out when you're full and as it runs out.
   - Now and then a hand comes with a **sacrifice**. Give up three quarters of your scrap and three more cards are
     dealt (the rarer ones likelier), and you take two of the six.
   - Rarer, a **curse**: half your stamina, healing at a quarter strength with worse bleeding (Bad blood:
     double scrap), a quarter of your health gone for good (shown in hazard stripes on your bar), slower reloads, hits
     that hurt half again as much (Fragile), or half the scrap from kills, for the rest of the run. Some never come
     together. In return, three legendary cards join the hand (legendary perks, or tiered skills with every tier at
     once), and you take two. Taking a curse sounds like one: a low, sour slide down, not the reward chime.
   - **No-perk mode** (Mode, on the title screen under View) takes all of that away: no cards, no aid, no sacrifices or
     curses, and no slow motion as a wave clears; a second after the last zombie falls the next break starts. In co-op
     it's picked in the lobby beside the view, by the host, for everyone.
4. **Repeat.** Each wave is bigger and meaner than the last, and pays a little more scrap. The run ends when you go down.
   No two waves come at quite the same pace: each keeps to a rhythm of its own, the usual surges with a few seconds'
   lull between them, a trickle of small ones close together, or a few big rushes with long quiet between. The banner's
   line under the wave number changes from wave to wave too, and its sting climbs a semitone every three waves with a
   drum beat more every four, so the night gets tenser as it goes; a hard wave (a twist, the brutes, a fog round) has
   a harsher one.
   - From wave 4, about one wave in three comes with a **twist**, never two running and never on a fog round or a
     brute wave, its name over the banner in red: a **Stampede** (a quarter more of the wave sprinting, a touch weaker,
     in big rushes), a **Swarm** (half as many again, weaker, in one long stream that never lets up) or a **Siege**
     (fewer, but over half as tough again, in a trickle that doesn't stop), and from wave 6 a **Blood moon** (as the
     night comes on the moonlight, the sky, the haze and the dark all go red, and elites come four times as often).
     Clearing one pays a bounty: six scrap plus the wave's number, to everyone.
   - **Elites.** From wave 3, now and then one of the horde is something more (about 1 in 28 at first, climbing to 1
     in 11; on a blood moon up to 3 in 10): a walker, a sprinter or a brute (never a spitter), its eyes burning its own
     colour and seen through cover in that colour. Every elite has a brute's hide (it takes 40% less damage; an elite
     brute 55% less), and each kind is far more than the zombie under it:
     - **Hulking** (violet): five times the health, bigger, and its hits land 1.8 times as hard; a little slower.
     - **Frenzied** (red): half again as fast, clawing 1.8 times as often, with three times the health.
     - **Volatile** (green, from wave 5: swollen, glowing sores all over it): three times the health, and a beat after
       it dies it bursts into a cloud of red mist that hangs and drifts for a few seconds, blood thrown all round it.
       It doesn't wait to be killed, either: one that gets within 1.7 m of whoever it's after swells and shudders with
       a shriek for 0.65 s and goes off on its own (in co-op the host's game decides it). Within 3.8 m it hurts you (up
       to 35, less at the edge), leaves you reeling (the screen swimming, your aim going wide, slowed) and throws its
       blood over your view and over you and your gun. It shell-shocks you too: stunned on the spot for a second (no
       moving, no shooting; a little less at its edge), the world blurred and drained nearly grey and clearing over about
       three seconds, the blood on your view sharp over it, and your ears ringing, a high, thin, beating tone over the
       muffled world that fades as your hearing comes back; the zombies within 4.4 m aren't hurt, only thrown back and
       staggered (up to 1.6 s at its middle, a brute barely moved). A volatile brute's burst is a third bigger.

     A Hulking walker takes about 27 pistol body shots where a walker takes 4, a Hulking brute about 92. A wave's elites
     are settled as it starts, so a moment after the wave's own call (3.4 s in) one card says how many it's bringing,
     with each kind side by side: its icon in its colour, how many and its name. The cards are kept out of the way: small,
     in the top right corner, sliding in from the right, in the game's own notification style (a see-through dark panel
     with the elite's colour down its edge, its icon in a tinted tile, a soft sweep of light as it lands and a thin bar
     running out under it). One put down gets a card of its own on every player's
     screen (the skull, its line and what it was) and a moment's hit-stop. Each pays scrap (6 to 8, more each wave), in
     the pickups on the left: the whole of it to anyone who did half its damage or more, and to anyone who did less, that
     much of it as a share of the half (a quarter of the damage, half the scrap), in whole scrap. Whoever killed it also
     gets an ammo box for sure, and about half the time bandages or planks too. In co-op an elite is the same one on every screen.
   - **A stray crate.** From wave 2, about one wave in five, somewhere 25 to 50 seconds in, a chopper drops a crate
     outside the fence. Its card drops in under the top of the screen in the supply orange: a ring round the crate's
     icon running down the 15 seconds till it lands, the seconds big beside it, the side of the farm it's coming down on
     (north, north-east...). It doesn't say what's coming for it. After a few seconds it folds down to a slim strip (the
     ring, "Crate inbound", the side as N/NE..., the seconds, ticking over the last three) that stays till it lands, then
     turns green as "Crate down" for a few seconds or till you open it. The drop marker still counts down with how far
     off it is. While it's inbound, sprinters come for it (4, and one more every 3 waves, a second or two apart, sent by
     whoever runs the zombies in co-op), in through the fence's gaps on the side it's coming down on (one of the two
     gaps nearest it). They're part of the wave from the moment it's called: the zombies-left count and the wave bar
     take them in at once, and the wave isn't over till they've all come and been killed. A crate you call on the radio
     yourself brings none. It comes down out in the yard between the fence and the
     tree line, clear of every tree's crown, the bushes and rocks, and anything else stood there (the cars, the hay
     bales): a radio crate's ammo, 2 bandages, 4 planks, a cache of scrap (8, and 2 more a wave) and a military find.
     In co-op each player gets their own. The helicopter itself flies over, along its shadow's line at the height the
     crate falls from: an olive transport with its tail boom, fin and skids, a two-bladed rotor turning so fast it's
     mostly a blur, coming in over the trees and going, seen from your own eyes as well as from above, for the radio's
     drops too.

**End game**, on the pause screen (it was Abandon run), ends the run there and then and goes to the end screen, after
asking. In the arena it's the host's: it ends the match for everyone and brings up the results and the scoreboard (in
a co-op run on the farm it isn't there, as before).

When the run ends the HUD clears away and the end screen comes up over the farm on its own; **Full run stats** folds open under your score: kills by zombie type and by weapon, headshots,
your longest kill and biggest chain, boards nailed and repaired, damage taken and your closest call (the lowest your health got that you lived through: a low only counts once you've
come through it and are still standing three seconds later, so the blow that finished you never does), scrap earned,
spent and sent to teammates, and every skill and perk you took.

## What's in it

**The farm.** Every run lays out a new house: its footprint, its rooms, its interior doors, its windows and where the
furniture stands, plus a new yard of cars, crates, hay bales and fences. The old rusted cars
have their hoods and trunks low below the windows: you see and shoot over those, and only the cabin is cover. The roof and walls fade away around you so
you can always see inside; from your own eyes the rooms have a proper ceiling over them instead, warm plaster with
stained timber joists running across the house, the lamps hanging from it on their cords. Indoors the roof keeps the
sun off everything under it, the floorboards' seams, the joists and everyone in the room (you, your own forearms from
your own eyes, and the dead) alike, so the rooms are lit by their lamps and the daylight through the windows, and out
in the yard you all go into the shade of the house and the trees. Furniture blocks sight, light and shots only where it really stands: a piece that
fills a little of its cell (the gun rack against the wall, a cabinet, a dresser) is measured to its own box, so the
part of the cell in front of it is open to see, light and shoot through, and the shadow it casts in the lighting is its
own shape, not the whole cell's, and you walk right up to it, bumping into the piece itself rather than the edge of its
cell. Only a piece filling most of its cell (85% or more each way) blocks the whole of it. The clock runs from afternoon into night, an hour every 30 seconds of a wave (so the sun and the moon drift across the sky rather than racing), and the dark is where the lamps, your
flashlight and the muzzle flashes matter. What the lamps don't reach isn't grey: the dark takes the cool of the sky,
a blue-violet moonlight by night and a cool blue-grey in the shade by day, and the shadows are graded cooler against the
warm of the light (with the lit colour a little fuller), so the honey daylight, the amber dusk and the lamplight have
something to stand against; by day the haze out over the woods is a cool blue-grey too, so the warm-lit farm stands
out from the distance. Each hour has its own grade (a lavender dawn, a violet dusk), eased between like the rest of
the light.

**The ground and the floors** are drawn from Screaming Brain Studios' Tiny Texture Packs: the farm's grasses, bare
earth and drive, the house's floorboards (four to the metre, their joints staggered), and every arena map's floor (the
concrete, asphalt, tiles, carpet, packed dirt, ploughed soil and barn straw: see each map below). They're laid in
metres across the world rather than stretched over a mesh, and never repeat where you'd see it: the loose ones
(concrete, asphalt, dirt) are copies turned and shifted patch by patch and faded into each other, the tiles shifted
a whole tile at a time so the grout still lines up, each board slid along its length its own way; a finer copy over
the loose ones keeps them crisp up close, and a slow drift of light and dark over all of it breaks up what's left.
Each is a small 128-pixel texture, mipmapped and filtered as finely as the graphics card allows, so they stay sharp
without shimmering from above and don't blur or go blocky from your own eyes. On a map they bring only their grain,
joints and grout: its colours, paint, oil and stains stay its own, and its lights, the dark, the flashlights and the
blood land on them as before. (tools/pack-ground.py packs them; world/floors.js lays them.)

**Guns.** A 9mm handgun, a .357 revolver, a .50 hand cannon, pump, double-barrel and auto shotguns, a hunting rifle, a
battle rifle, a machine pistol, a submachine gun, an assault rifle and a light machine gun. Each one has its own
reload: mags in and out, shells one at a time, speed loaders and belts. The LMG's belt is a real chain of linked
rounds, simulated: each round hinged to the next by its link (two loops round each case, bridged over to the next
round's) at the belt's pitch, bending freely in its own plane up to where the rounds would touch, a little stiff, with
only a little give fore and aft. It comes out of the feed port flat along a short feed tray under the cover's lip, over
the tray's edge and down into the box, the rest of it lying on the stack folded away in there. It's stepped at a fixed
240 steps a second whatever the frame rate (drawn between its last two steps), in the gun's frame, feeling the gun's
motion through space as its own inertia: it swings and settles as you walk, turn, sprint, aim and reload, never
stretches, and keeps off the box's walls, lip and floor, the tray and the receiver's side. Every shot pulls it a link
on into the gun, the next round taking the port and the rest following over the tray's edge and up out of the box, so
a burst visibly runs the belt in. Once fewer rounds are left than the belt shows, its end comes up out of the box and
hangs off the tray, shorter with every shot. In a reload the cover's opened as the old box comes off (the belt let go),
the fresh box comes up with its end lying loose in it, and once the box is on the end's laid over the lip onto the tray
and drawn into the port. A teammate sees each round where yours are. At the workbench you can add sights, lasers,
suppressors (on every gun but the double barrel and the revolver: the revolver's barrel upgrade is its longer barrel, 4", 6" then 8", in the workbench and in the arena's loadouts alike, and it never takes a can), stocks, grips and underbarrel grenades (a laser's beam is a fine line of light from its lens to its dot, the same width on screen however you look along it, never a flat strip), and change calibres with conversion kits such as AK mags for the
battle rifle (rocked in front lug first, the AK way) or 10mm for the SMG. The double barrel's sawn-off kit cuts it to a coach gun: more pellets in a wider spread, each hitting 30% harder up close, but its short barrels drop it off hard (full damage only to 2.5 m, down to a fifth of it by 7 m, where the full-length gun holds to 4 m and keeps half past 11). Down the sights it's quicker on its feet than the full-length gun too (76% of your pace against 66%). Every slug, out of any shotgun, holds its damage far further than shot but still tails off: full damage to 10 m, easing down to 60% by 26 m and never below that. Out of the sawn-off a slug drops off hard instead, as its buckshot does: full damage to 3 m and down to a fifth by 8 m. The double barrel itself was too strong early on: it's now eight pellets of 16 a barrel (from nine of 19: 256 for both barrels, from 342), its second barrel follows a touch slower (0.12 s) and each shell takes 0.5 s to load (from 0.42); still the biggest burst up close, not by so much. You can also load special rounds (hollow points, slugs, flechettes and more) and
tune damage, fire rate, recoil and magazine size. The hand cannon's mags, the first extension and the longest alike,
are straight sticks, no curve at all, carrying on down out of the grip's foot as one piece with it: flush with the
grip's steel front and back where they meet, as wide as it, its top tucked up inside the grip so there's no gap or
step (it used to hang below the grip at its own angle, its top tilted against the grip's foot), leaning back on the
front strap's rake, a pressed panel down each side of the long one, and the gun's own floor plate, lip out front,
moved down to its foot; the
machine pistol's magazine steps go from its stick to an extended stick and then straight to a drum (no casket). The
shotguns' chokes are real choke tubes, as wide as the muzzle they thread into: the **Modified choke** (tier 1) a black
tube sitting almost flush with a castellated rim, the **Full choke** (tier 2) an extended steel tube standing out past
the muzzle with a knurled grip band, each with its dark bore (the double barrel's in both barrels). They tighten the
spread now, and say by how much: the Modified choke cuts buckshot's spread by 30% and a slug's by 45%; the Full choke
by 51% and 70% (`CHOKE_SPREAD` in `game/run-state.js`). The double barrel's **twin trigger** and its **sawn-off** kit
are a single step each now. The hand cannon's
bore is a full round hole out past its bevelled muzzle face, and the MP5's butt pad is closed at its foot. The LMG
takes optics now (it took none): a red dot, a holo, the scope or the ACOG, nothing bigger, on a picatinny rail along its
feed cover as an M60E4's, over the bore and well clear of the belt and tray, its ladder sight folded flat under it; the
workbench, the arena's loadouts and its random kits all offer them. On the hunting rifle every optic goes on a picatinny rail along the
receiver, like the other guns: the red dot, the holo and the ACOG straight on it, the scopes in picatinny rings clamped
to it, both rings on the front of the receiver ahead of the ejection port, so nothing stands over the bolt (the barrel's
rear notch comes off for the rail). An optic on a handgun's slide (the handgun's red dot or holo, the hand cannon's) rides back with the slide only a quarter as far while you're down the sights, so each shot no longer throws the sight back into your eye (the kick still rocks the whole gun). The hand cannon takes sights up to the ACOG: the red dot and the holo on its slide,
and a handgun scope or the ACOG on the rail along its barrel (which stays put while the slide works), held out at arm's
length with a handgun's long eye relief rather than up against your eye. An optic on the auto shotgun goes up on its rear
sight, as the real AA-12's does: a short picatinny rail across the top of the tall A-frame rear sight, running on
forward over the receiver, braced back down to it by a triangular steel plate with a triangle cut out of it. Both iron
sights stay up under it, so the optic sits high over the gun and the front post shows low in its window. A suppressor heats up the longer you keep firing through it, a little with each round, so it takes a
good many mags of sustained fire to get it hot (a full mag barely warms it), and it cools off slowly once you stop
(just for the look). A hotter powder warms it a little quicker: hand-loads about 8% more a round, +P loads 16%,
match-grade 24%. Warm, the
barrel smokes, thin wisps curling up off the muzzle with a puff at each shot; hotter, the whole can, its bands and
clamp too, glows a dull red, then orange, then a deep yellow at its hottest (it never washes out to white), with a slight
yellow bloom round it; and overheating, the smoke pours
off it slow and heavy and hangs in the air, so moving or swinging the gun leaves a trail of it behind. Left alone it
fades back down through the same colours, still smoking a while. However fast you fire, the smoke keeps coming: with a lot of smoke and
dust in the air at once, it's the faintest, nearly faded puff that's reused for a new one, so nothing that's still
plainly there ever blinks out.

**Melee.** An ash bat, crowbar, pipe wrench, machete, katana, fire axe and sledgehammer, each with its own swings,
reach and stamina cost. Sharp blades take heads and limbs off. The bat is one smooth turned piece of ash with eight nails
already driven through its barrel (a scavenger's bat, never a bare one); its Nails mod drives in more, real
nails driven right through the barrel, round heads on the wood and points out the far side, and Barbed wire, its own
mod now (no nails needed), is two twisted strands wound up the barrel with barbs every few centimetres. The crowbar is
one red hex bar, the steel worn bright at both ends: a short angled pry foot with a nail slot, the long shaft and a
tight gooseneck running straight into a flattened claw split into two prongs, with no gap where the bar meets it. The katana's curved blade is one piece, its spine, hamon and edge running unbroken to the point, and it's held by
its wrapped handle, both hands on the grip below the guard. From your own eyes a melee weapon in your hands keeps your
body together with your legs, and a swing doesn't carry you forward. From your own eyes each swing is kept where you
can see it: the wind-up stays at the top of the view rather than behind your head, your arm never sweeps through your
face, and the blow crosses the view. The weapon is swung round by your arm and held in the fist as it is in the guard,
so it never spins about its handle or flips over in your hand, and the forearm's roll and the weapon's tilt ease in
and out of every turn instead of starting and stopping dead. Through a swing the edge (the axe's bit, a blade's edge)
faces round the way the swing turns: leading the blow, trailing as it's drawn back, still leading as it carries on
past and comes back round, so the axe's head stays upright through an overhead chop all the way down, from your own
eyes and from above, never turned flat on its side at the bottom or flipped over about the handle.

**Every kill lands.** A round or a blow of yours that hits flashes a marker on your crosshair: four short strokes on
the diagonals, white, gold through the head, and a bigger red one for a kill. A hit down the sights in first person
(where the crosshair's put away) marks nothing, so no marker turns up when you come off the sights, however soon. A kill also ticks, a short bright note
that climbs a semitone with each kill in a chain, and shakes the view by how hard it landed: a light round a nudge, a
heavy round, a shotgun or a heavy blow much more, through the head or with a big overkill more again. Playing alone, a
kill that lands hard (through the head with anything but a fast automatic, a heavy round, a shotgun, a heavy blow)
holds the world still for a few hundredths of a second, never closer together than a third of a second, so a run of
kills doesn't stutter. Kills in a chain (each within 1.6 seconds of the last) are called out on a small card above the
middle, Double kill to Unstoppable: a pip for each kill in the chain, the scrap it's paid, and a thin bar draining over
the time left to keep it going. Each kill pops it in again with its newest pip bursting in, its glow hotter the longer
the chain runs, and it fades as soon as the chain can't go on. A long chain pays a little scrap as it grows: one at its 3rd, 5th and 7th kills, then one every 5 kills from the
10th (a chain of 10 pays 4, of 20 pays 6), not a scrap for every kill in it.
A kill sounds like what made it: through the head the skull bursting (two recordings), through the body it giving
way with a deep thud as it drops (four), heavier for a blade or a club, a hiss for a burning one; most of the time
with the zombie's last breath going out of it. Bullets into flesh have four recordings rather than two. The new
recordings are from the pack already in use (`assets/sounds/Guns and stuffs.zip`, cut by `tools/make-sounds.mjs`).

**Their voices.** Every zombie has a voice of its own, pitched for it when it turns up: a walker anywhere from a low
moan to a reedy one, a sprinter's a ragged shriek that climbs (and it lets one out as it comes out of the treeline,
most of the time), a brute's a deep, rasping roar (as it charges, too), a spitter's a wet gurgle. They're heard from
where they are, quieter and off to the side with distance and muffled through the walls (every wall, tall prop and
shut door or window between you counts, up to three, each muffling it more; but only what's fully between you: a
sound that can get round a wall's end or past it through a gap beside it comes through, only a little softened, so
one just round a corner is clear), and it's now one, now
another of the few nearest you, more often the more of them are close by, so a crowd round the house moans over
itself.

**Fog rounds.** Once in a while (never within three waves of the last) a wave comes in under a thick ground fog that rolls in off the fields. It's a real bank
of fog with height: densest low down and thinning up to a billowing top that rises and falls across the yard, with
thicker and thinner patches through it, paler at its tops and darker in its depths. From above, taller things (a tree,
the burn barrel, a fence post) stand up out of it and its billows shift as the view moves; from your own eyes it's a
wall of mist at waist and head height with the tree tops and the sky showing over it, brightening looking toward the
sun. It stays out of the house, parts round you in a ring that opens a little wider higher up, and anything out in it
can't be seen, outlined or picked out by your crosshair until it's nearly on you. Lamps and the fire glow in it, and
your flashlight's beam lights it up and cuts a clear path through it. Smoke, sparks and the like near you aren't
buried under the fog beyond them: each shows as much as it would standing where it is (clear in the open ring round
you, gone deep out in the fog), and a sight's reticle always shows through it. The fog is worked out at a third of the
screen's resolution (a ninth of the pixels, its dearest part by far; from above, where a look crosses only its shallow
layer, with fewer steps through it too) and brought up to full size by depth, so the edge of anything standing in front
of it stays clean. Down a scope it's there just as it is round it, worked out for the scope's own view. A zombie only
faintly there doesn't hide the fog behind it, so one all but lost in the fog never shows as a clear patch in its shape.

**Zombies.** Shambling walkers, fast runners, spitters that lob acid at anyone who stands still (the splat it leaves is
nearly four metres across and lasts fourteen seconds: a bubbling green pool, bubbles swelling all over it and popping
in bright rings under an oily swirl, glowing on its own at night; standing in it burns for 2 every half second, rising
to 6 after four seconds in it, and drags at your feet), and brutes that
charge through fences and plough through the crowd: from 4 to 11 m off with a clear line to you, a brute crouches and
charges more often than not within a second or two (and again 6 to 10 s after), a red lane on the ground showing where. Every zombie it ploughs into is knocked off its feet (one square in its path as the brute reaches it, one it clips thrown off to the side it was on), lies there a moment and gets back up; only a crawler is just shoved along the ground. (They used to stay frozen where they fell: two files had a function of the same name, and in the one script the player's gun-kick spring had replaced the zombies' knockdown step. The build now fails on any two top-level functions with the same name.) Their hits are gentle on the first nights: on wave 1 a zombie hits
for 55% of its full damage (a walker for about 16 out of your 100 health, not 29), rising evenly to full by wave 8,
and from there each wave hits 3% harder as before. You walk right up to the fence: it stops you at its rails and posts, not a stride short of them. Shots land where they hit: a leg shot slows them, a severed leg
leaves a crawler (lying low along the ground, its hips and what's left of its legs dragging, its chest propped on its
forearms), and a headshot drops them. A zombie that's lost a limb bleeds out from the stump, slowly: 2.5 health a
second for a whole arm or leg, 1.5 for a forearm or shin, up to 7.5 however many it's lost, dripping blood as it goes
and with no damage numbers. Blood on the ground stains the ground and what stands fixed on it, never you or a teammate standing in it. A shotgun's load (buckshot, a slug or flechettes; not
dragon's breath) blows a limb to red mist when enough of one blast lands on it: three pellets on an arm or a leg (four
flechettes, or a single slug), however much of it was left, gone where it was in a cloud of red with scraps of it
tumbling away. A kill through the head with any of them blows the head to mist every time from up close, and further
off when two or more pellets went into it. Close by (within about 3 m), a limb or a head coming off or blown apart,
your doing or a teammate's, spatters your view with blood, as if on your face, thrown from the side it happened on: a
faint red haze from that edge, a spray of fine specks, wobbly-edged drops (deep red, darker where it pools at the rim,
thinner in the middle, a soft sheen and a small glint) and, from a close one, a splat or two with spikes and beads flung
out of them, all soft and out of focus. The bigger drops run down leaving a thin trail, and it fades in a couple of
seconds. It gets on you and what's in your hands as well (the same stain as from fighting; each splash of blood on you
or a weapon stays 30 seconds and then fades out over 3, and a cleared wave cleans you up anyway): a limb blown to mist
throws about twice what a clean cut does, and a volatile going off near you more than either. It's the same few a second on any night, so on later nights, with more health, they take
longer to bleed out. Dragon's breath sets a zombie alight where its pellets hit (not where they hit armour: a helmet with its face shield
down, a vest, a plate carrier or leg pads stop them catching there, and armour stops more of dragon's breath than of
plain buckshot, about 60% more): the flames start on that arm, leg or
the head and spread out over the rest of it in about a second and a half, its orange rim glowing up and the charring
spreading with them. A shotgun blast that takes a head doesn't knock it off whole: it bursts into a red
mist, flinging chips of skull, scalp and gore. Blood thrown from a hit flies as teardrops, round head first with a tail
drawn out behind (longer the faster it goes), each flattened its own way, and leaves its mark where it lands; only the
drops in the air cost anything to draw, so a shotgun on a crowd stays smooth. Blood thrown at a wall lands on the face it
struck, never the far side: thrown at it head-on it's a burst, a heavy blot with droplets flung out all round it;
slantwise, a spatter streaking the way the blood was going, each drop drawn out into a teardrop with a tail. Runs creep
down the wall from the heaviest of it over a few seconds, slowing as they go, a bead swelling at the foot of each. It's
a wet red fresh and dries to a dull brown (never drawn on a drop or a supply crate lying on the ground), and soaks away on the ground's clock (faster once the wave's over); a kill
throws more of it, and a blast paints the walls all round. A splash keeps to the wall it's on: what would hang past the
end of the wall, or over its top, is cut away, and where the wall turns a corner the rest of it folds round onto the next
face, round the outside of a corner or into an inside one, so it reads as one splash wrapped round the corner instead of
a sticker floating in the air past it. Its runs keep to the wall too. Each splash is a single lit quad on the wall (at most 48 of
them and 64 runs at once), and fades with its wall when the wall's cut away for you to see in. Their hitboxes are simple shapes, not the model itself: a round meets a
zombie as an upright cylinder and hits the part at the height it passes (head, body or arm, legs), and from above the
part under your crosshair (the one your first round goes to) is found against one box per limb, fitted round the limb
and everything it wears and turning with it, only for zombies near the line of your aim and once a frame. From wave 4 some turned wearing armour: soldiers in helmets and vests,
SWAT in plate carriers, and riot officers whose face shields blunt headshots; every helmet counts, even a builder's
hard hat or a fireman's, which stop only a little. A helmet without a face shield only guards the top of the head: a
round in the face, under the brim, goes round it. Armour soaks up part of each hit where it's worn (heavier rounds, slugs and piercing rounds go through more
of it, and the .50 hand cannon and the battle rifle (not once converted to AK mags) punch through far more of it), thin early on and thicker in later waves and against a better gun, and those hits show in steel with a
shield. A melee blow depends on what it lands with: a blunt one (the bat, the crowbar, the wrench, the sledge) jars
through most of it, a spiked one (nails in the bat, spikes on the sledge) catches on it, and an edge (the machete, the
katana, the axe) mostly glances off. Enough hits crack a piece, and then it splits and falls off. The dead fall as ragdolls,
thrown further the harder they were put down (up to 70% further for a big overkill: a heavy round through a walker
flings it where a light one drops it), and a kill flashes the body brighter than a hit does. The ragdolls were rebuilt to be far
less buggy: a body eases out of the exact pose it died in (no jump at the moment of death, for zombies, bots and you),
walls push a falling body out along their surface instead of snapping it back (no more stretched limbs or bodies flung
off a wall), the fence is its real posts and rails so a body folds over the rail, furniture, props and trees use their
real shape, the floor holds a body with proper friction (no skating), knees and elbows no longer twitch against the
floor, nothing starts faster than 7 m/s, and every body settles within about 2.4 s of landing, eased to a stop rather
than frozen mid-motion. Measured over 84 deaths: the worst limb stretch went from 86% to 13%, bodies left floating from
3 to none. **A head split by a blade** (zombie or, in the arena, a survivor) is cut by the plane through it: each half
keeps only its own eye and its half of the nose, mouth, hair and beard, and the cut face shows the skull, brain, teeth
and meat; an axe splits straight down or across, a katana or machete sometimes on a slant. A round into the ground
kicks up a fountain of dirt clods and a puff of dust with no sparks; into a wooden wall it throws splinters and only a
spark or two; a metal grate still sparks.
Brutes come in slowly: the first brute wave sends just one, in plain clothes with no armour on it, and each wave after
lets a few more in.

**Holding the house.** Nail up to three boards over every window and door, and repair them between waves. Upgrade the
sawhorse to a metalwork bench and bar the openings with steel grates instead, reinforced in five tiers. Once a grate is
plated, a wide firing slit is cut through the plate at first-person eye height, so from your own eyes you can see out
along it and shoot through it (zombies outside show through it too). A grate that's torn out throws its plates and bars
clear of the wall, into the room or outside depending on which way it's pulled, never down into the wall itself, and
every piece of it (and every board and flake) comes to rest lying on the floor at its own thickness rather than sunk
into it. Vault through
windows and hop fences to escape (only with the fence right in front of you, within about a metre of it and where
there's fence to go over, not past the end of a run or beside a gap; and a window only from within about 65 cm of its
middle along the wall), and shove zombies off you when they get close. A shove with a gun is a short wind-up and a blow with the gun itself, gathering speed into the blow rather than snapping out, and easing back to the aim. Shove after shove wears your arms out: the first four in a row come as quick as ever, then each one more waits 30% longer (up to 2.2 times as long) and is itself a touch slower; stop shoving for two seconds and it comes back, two shoves' worth a second. An elite isn't so easily moved: a shove throws it back less than half as far, and it reels for half as long.

Every gun's, mod's and attachment's description says plainly what it is and does, with no advice on how to use it.

Buying something you can't afford (or can't have yet) answers with a short dull two-note "no" and the button shaking,
the quick ammo and bandage buttons included, rather than the click a station makes when it opens. In a short window
the station's panel comes down over the HUD to give its list room. The big banners ("Night 1", a wave starting) wait

shown at all if you've already started the first wave. The co-op lobby scrolls itself into view when it opens in a

**The menus.** The title, pause and end screens list what you can do one under another in the big worn type, and
the one a key starts (or the one under the pointer or the keyboard's focus) has a strip of blaze-orange surveyor's
flagging tape behind it, torn off the roll at its end; the tape is the theme colour, so it changes with it. The
controls on the title are a sheet of paper taped up beside the story, like the cards you pick after a wave, each key a
dark inked cap on it. The menus' and the settings' own text is set in Archivo, in sentence case, with the typed lines
in Special Elite; the view picker under the story is the two views as words, the one picked underlined with the tape.

**Co-op.** Up to four players on the farm, six in the arena. On the title screen press **Co-op**: its own screen
opens over the title, with your name at the top, your game on the left and your friends down the right (Esc or
**Back** goes back to the title). The ways in are three cards side by side, each with its picture, what it does in a
line or two, a few tags, and its button at the foot, all three level: **Host a game** (orange), **Join a game**
(blue) and **Arena against bots** (red). They come in one after another as the screen opens, and lift under the
pointer, their edge in their colour. Press **Open a lobby** to host, or type the host's four-letter code under **Join a game** (pasted in lower case or
with a space, it's tidied up for you); friends of yours who are hosting with room are listed beside them, a click from
joining. In a lobby:

- **The seats** are four cards pinned in a row, each with that survivor's picture as they've made them, their number
  and name, Host and You.
- **Ready.** A joiner presses **Ready** (or T) when they're set, and a green Ready stamp lands on their card. They can
  take it back with **Cancel ready**. The host's **Start together** (or T) waits until everyone is ready and says who
  it's waiting for; a host on their own can start straight away.
- **The host** picks the view everyone plays in, **Isometric** or **First person** (the others see the pick but can't
  change it, and their own choice from the title comes back once they leave the game). The host can send someone out
  of the lobby with the cross on their card, and **Close the lobby** ends it for everyone.
- **Inviting.** **Copy code** copies the four letters, and **Copy invite link** copies a link that opens the game
  straight into the co-op screen and joins.
- **Chat.** The lobby has a chat: type under the log and press Enter. The lobby's own goings-on (who joined, who
  left, the view changing) show among the lines in grey. In the run, press Enter to say something to your team: the
  last few lines show under the wave box and fade a few seconds after they're said, and Esc puts the box away.
- **Friends.** Add a friend by their six-character friend code (they're added back). The list shows who's around and
  what they're doing: on the title, in a lobby, hosting and how full, or in a run and on which wave. You can join a
  friend's lobby or invite a friend into yours. An invite shows at the top right of their title screen with **Join**
  and **Not now**, whether or not they have co-op open. Everyone you've been in a lobby with is kept under
  **Played with recently**, a click from becoming a friend. Removing a friend asks once more before it happens.

Each player keeps their own weapons, scrap, drops and cards. If
you go down, a low drone drifts in while you watch a teammate until the wave is cleared, then come back as a new
survivor with a full mag in each gun and three spares. While you watch, the picture is drained of colour and grainy,
like an old camera feed, with faint scan lines and a brighter band rolling slowly down it, so it's plain you're out
(with reduced motion turned on in your system, the grain holds still). The panel for the teammate you watch shows
their weapon as your own HUD does: a melee weapon shows its picture where a gun's round count would be, with the upgrades they've put on it (nails in the bat, and so on, at their levels). In a
first-person game you watch from their own eyes: the camera is where theirs is, looking where they look, as wide as they
see, with their arms and weapon posed as they see them (their head hidden out of the lens's way, their name tag gone,

take, someone they shoot or cut close up, a head bursting near them, thrown in from the side it happened on against the

scrap. When a co-op run ends you're back in its lobby, with the title's Co-op marked by a green dot while you're in one.

**The arena.** In a co-op lobby the host can pick **Arena** under **Game** in place of **Survival**: players against
each other on a map of its own, in either view (the lobby's **Isometric** or **First person**). There are no zombies,
waves, scrap, stations or cards, and the farm's HUD that goes with them (the wave and clock, planks, scrap and kills,
the pickups feed) is put away. The host picks one of three modes:

- **Deathmatch.** Everyone for themselves, first to 15 kills (the host can change it: Match settings, below). Go down and you're back in 4.5 seconds at the spawn
  point furthest from everyone else, and you can change class while you wait.
- **Team deathmatch.** Two sides, Blue and Red, as the lobby has them (Sides, below). Each side
  starts at its own end and respawns there. The first side to 20 kills wins.
- **Showdown.** Two sides (1v1 up to 3v3), one life a round, first side to 5 rounds. Health doesn't come back:
  what you lose stays lost till the next round. Go down and the view dips to black and comes up on a teammate still
  standing, stepping between them with Q and E, bots included, and from their own eyes in first person. Once your
  whole side is down, the camera goes to the enemy who took the last of you down (whoever killed your last teammate,
  or whoever killed you if you were the last): the death camera follows them as in the other modes, then you watch from
  their eyes in first person (from above if that's your view). The strip at the top says so plainly: an **Enemy** stamp
  in place of **Down**, "Watching the enemy" and their name in red, on a red-tinted strip. If they've gone down too
  (a trade), you watch whichever of their side still standing is nearest where they fell; Q and E step through the rest
  of their side. The last side standing takes the round.
  - **The flag.** Once the round's clock runs out (1:00 to start with), a flag goes up in the middle of the plaza, a
    ring on the ground round it. Stand inside the ring with nobody from the other side in it and your side's share
    builds; 5 seconds of it all told (the capture time) takes the round. The more of your side on it, the faster it
    builds: one alone at the normal rate, two 1.6 times as fast, three 2.4 times. With both sides in the ring it holds; while
    your side's off it, what you had slips back at half the rate. A bar under the score shows each side's share
    filling in from its own end, who's on it, and the flag's own clock: the capture time plus 10 seconds (15 to start
    with). If that runs
    out first, the side with the most health left between them takes the round; level, it's a draw. The flag turns
    your side's colour while you're taking it.
  - **Random loadouts** (the default; the host can switch Showdown to the classes). Everyone carries the same: one
    primary (any of the long guns or automatics) and one sidearm (handgun, revolver or hand cannon), each with
    attachments and mods drawn at random (a scope, a laser, a can, a choke, a speed loader, a lighter trigger, bigger
    mags; Slam fire, the 10mm kit, the AK conversion, a second pistol, burst and so on), and a melee weapon. Each is
    drawn at any of its levels, 1 up to as far as that line goes on the gun (a level II or III can, a scope up to the
    gun's strongest, a mod at level I to III), but never the powder, never a tune-up and never a longer belt on the
    LMG (a loadout kept from before with one goes in with the stock belt). A mod that needs another first (the auto
    sear needs the burst conversion) used to be left out of the draw altogether, so full auto never turned up; now
    when the draw gives a handgun the burst conversion, it adds the auto sear 60% of the time (with the burst
    conversion at the level it needs), and burst itself comes up more often (60%). The same set lasts two rounds
    (the host can make it 1, 2 or 3: Match settings), then a whole new one is drawn. A round with a new set shows it
    on a card for 3 seconds, the round's number on it; a round that keeps last round's skips the card and goes straight
    to the count-in, and your guns aren't drawn again for it. There's no picking, and no perks. **With each new
    loadout the sides swap ends,** as at half time (the count-in says so): Blue starts from the east yard, Red the west,
    and back again with the next.
  Between rounds everyone is back at their spawn, whole again (and with the classes, the chance to change class).
  **Blood** in Showdown stays where it fell all round, none of it soaking away, and is gone the moment the next round
  starts.

**Sides.** In the team modes the lobby's seats are grouped Blue then Red, each marked with its side. Someone joining
goes to the side with fewer on it. Anyone can move themselves to the other side (the side's badge in the corner of

**The lobby's chat** slides each new line up into place and glides the log down to it.

**Bots.** Not enough people? In an arena lobby the host can sit bots in the empty seats with **Add a bot**, and send

can join it, and it's always the arena. The host picks how good the bots are under **Bots**: Recruit, Regular,
Veteran, **Terminator**, or **Random**, where each bot is drawn its own skill of the first three as it sits down (its
seat shows which; Random never deals a Terminator, that's only ever picked on purpose). A bot's seat
has its kick cross clear of its side's badge.

**Reacting** takes longer the more of a surprise you are: off to the side of where it's looking (up to 70% longer at
the edge of what it sees), someone it had no idea of (a third longer), and that close, round a corner under 8 m (a
quarter longer again); its first aim then starts further off you. Someone it knew was just there it reacts to quicker.

**Every bot plays to its gun.** A bot with a shotgun or a double barrel (anything short-ranged) never peeks out at
someone more than about 15% past its gun's reach: it flanks round to them (half the time) or holds its corner for them
to come close instead. Spotted from further off than it can hit, it goes back into cover rather than stand in the
open. Out of shells (or with a double barrel's two spent), it ducks back behind cover to reload, and from cover it
takes a pop shot when someone's in reach and goes straight back in. The other guns keep to their own ranges as before.

To every game in the match a bot is just another player. It picks a class, carries that class's guns and bandages,
counts in the score, the kill feed and the results, can be spectated, and bleeds and falls like anyone else. The
host's game runs the bots.

A bot has no wall hacks:

- It sees you only when you're in front of it (or right beside it), within its range, with nothing in the way. Low
  cover like sandbags doesn't hide you.
- It hears gunfire up to 34 m away (45 m in Deathmatch and Team deathmatch), or 9 m for a suppressed gun, and footsteps (a sprint to 16 m, a walk to 8 m, crouched about a third of that). It only gets a
  rough idea of where the sound came from, rougher the further off it was.
- A round that goes by it (within about 2 m) it takes as being shot at, and knows just where it came from.
- It sees a laser's beam: if any of the beam (up to 24 m, stopping at the first wall) is in front of it and in plain
  sight, it knows roughly where it's coming from.
- When it's hit, it knows roughly where the shot came from.
- **Call-outs.** In the team modes a bot that sees you tells its side's other bots where you are. They don't react as
  if they'd seen you themselves, but they know where to look and where to go.
- It remembers where it last saw or heard each foe, and when, for 14 seconds. **Someone who's shot at it** (a hit or a
  near miss) it remembers for 40 seconds, and keeps its gun on that spot.

What it does with that:

**Deadlier (this batch).** Bots react quicker (recruit 0.34–0.55 s, regular 0.22–0.36 s, veteran 0.13–0.22 s), settle
their aim faster, and their first two rounds after a breath spread 40% less.
- **Hold the trigger** on automatics: 12–22 rounds inside 9 m, 5–9 at mid range, 3–5 far, taps past 28 m; the auto
  shotgun goes full auto up close (5–8 shells under 7 m, 2–4 out to 13 m). Kick builds while they hold it and better
  bots ride it better.
- **Go for the kill.** They prefer someone they've hurt and someone looking at them, push in on a hurt target firing
  as they walk, chase at once when it breaks sight, and won't break off to heal while it's in reach. In the team
  modes, when a teammate goes down nearby they swing on the killer together (a trade); in Deathmatch they hear two
  others fighting and come in from the side to **third-party** them, keeping out of both fighters' sight on the way.
- **Prefire.** When whoever they were fighting ducks out of sight, they hold that corner and put 2–4 rounds into it a
  moment later (more likely the better they are), never through a wall; and someone coming out where one's aimed is
  met quicker.
- **Pre-aim, not walls.** On the move they look down the angles a foe could come from (a doorway's edge, the end of a
  sightline opening past a corner, where foes were last heard), never at a wall within 3 m; a known foe out of sight
  has its corner covered.
- **Turn faster**, 3.6 / 4.6 / 5.6 rad/s at the top by tier, and **shot**, they whip round on the shooter after a short
  beat (0.08–0.32 s by tier) at 1.7 times that, eased in and out rather than snapping.
- **Walk at full pace** everywhere but a fight (only slowing for sights fighting, peeking, in cover or near a foe they
  knew of a moment ago), and sprint more on the way to fights, trades and chases.
- **Peek with the edge**: a wide swing at full pace when they have it (healthy, you hurt or looking away, a teammate on
  you too), a quick jiggle when they don't; back out of cover a moment after a reload, or the sidearm out instead of
  reloading with you close.
- **Fewer pauses**: they heal later (22 health in Deathmatch, 28 in Showdown, lying low only 2–3.5 s), hold corners and
  standoffs about half as long, and in Showdown more of each side runs its lane at the start and they rush more.
- **Crouching works on them**: someone crouched behind low cover is unseen past 4 m and their shots meet the cover.
- Their hits and kills carry what's on their guns and their assists, for the kill feed.

**Veterans, and playing like people (this batch).**
- **Veterans are genuinely hard now** (Recruit and Regular play as before): reactions 0.10–0.17 s, a tighter first aim
  that settles faster, about half the spread, a steadier hand on a held trigger, and they lead someone strafing rather
  than trailing them. In a fight they cut back and forth unevenly, bob in a crouch, go for whoever's out in the open
  over whoever's behind cover, press someone they've just slowed with a hit, and get out of the open when they're
  tagged themselves.
- **No moonwalking.** A bot on the move keeps its aim within about 100° of the way it's going, unless it's holding an
  angle (in cover, peeking, waiting in ambush) or has just seen someone. It used to backpedal at a run with its gun
  on something behind it, its legs sliding the wrong way under it.
- **Hits slow bots as they slow you**: about half pace from a hit, a third from a heavy one, sprint broken, worn off over
  a second and a half or two.
- **Footsteps.** A bot hears a sprint out to 16 m and a walk to 8 m; crouch-walking is about a third as loud. Hearing
  you coming round a corner, it gets ready by coming out at you first, a peek or a swing round the corner. (It used to
  crouch where it was and wait with its gun on the corner half the time, so any corner you were heard coming round had
  a bot sat behind it; it no longer does.)
- **Leans, quick peeks, new angles.** Peeking, bots lean out round their cover (head and shoulder, not the whole body;
  veterans nearly always), fire a burst and pull back in rather than holding the angle, and come again from somewhere
  else (a wide swing, the other side, or round another way) rather than peeking the same spot twice.
- **Crouch to shoot straighter** far off (or with a gun in each hand some way off), as you do: crouching steadies a bot
  just as it steadies you, and a crouched bot's harder to hit, behind low cover hidden from other bots.
- **Down the sights they look it**: the head tips over onto the stock and the face screws up to aim, from outside and
  from their own eyes when you spectate.
- **Top off in a lull**: nobody seen or shooting at them for a couple of seconds, they reload a part-empty mag
  (veterans almost any time, recruits only when it's half gone), a veteran stepping into cover first; topping off with
  rounds still in it, they drop the reload to fight anyone who turns up.
- **Use the whole map.** Each bot takes a lane (the two sides as often as the middle) worked out from the map itself, on
  every map, and on a side lane it keeps out of the middle on long walks; more of them work the outer edges.
- **Showing off.** Now and then (the cockier the bot, the likelier) a bot that's made a kill with nobody else about walks
  over the body and crouches on it a few times. Counting in, and walking about after a round, some look their gun over
  as you can (your own inspect moves), letting it go part way to get moving.

**Terminator: harder than Veteran, everything cranked up.** A fifth pick under **Bots** (pvp/bots.js, the fourth row of
BOT_SKILL and the Terminator's own knobs on it):
- **Lasers you from far off.** It reacts in 0.13–0.2 s (quick, not superhuman), its first aim is about a quarter as far off you as a veteran's
  and settles twice as fast, it turns a little quicker than a veteran, and it sees out to 75 m across a wider view. Its shots
  hardly spread: a fifth of the spread its own moving and its gun's looseness add, a twelfth of what range adds, and
  almost none from a held trigger. It fires 70% further past each gun's usual reach.
- **Mostly headshots.** It aims at the head, and about four in five of the hits it lands that way are in the head (a
  veteran's land there at most half the time). Low cover still takes its rounds as it does anyone's.
- **Quick-peeks everything.** Its peeks, the gaps between them and its time holding a corner are all about half as long.
  After a burst from a stepped-out peek it's straight back in behind its cover, as it already was from a lean, and comes
  again from somewhere else.
- **Crouches and leans.** Once a fight's on it bobs in a crouch about half the time (less than it used to), each bob a
  tenth to a fifth of a second. It also leans one way and the other every fifth to a third of a second, always to a side
  it can still see you from, at any walking pace and quicker to lean over than anyone else.
- **Runs everywhere.** It sprints wherever it's going (looking, flanking, searching, going after someone, out to its
  first spot of a round) and never walks in quietly. It still puts its gun up to fight, as you have to.
- **Knows the angles and prepares (Showdown).** The bots keep a heat map of where the other side comes through, one
  for each side, over the match. At the start of each round it's seeded with the other side's likely ways from their spawns
  up each of the three lanes, walked on one of the game's worker threads (below). From then on it adds every place one
  of them is first seen or shoots from, which counts for more. It fades by a quarter each round, so what you did lately
  counts most. Knowing of nobody, a Terminator runs to a spot in its own half that watches the most of that heat, the
  more of it in one direction the better. It prefers a spot beside something it can lean round, and nearer is better.
  There it holds with its gun on the angle, crouched if there's low cover in front, leaned round what's beside it.
  Hearing or being told of someone coming the way it's watching, it stays on it for them.
- **Repositions if nobody comes.** After 5 to 8.5 seconds with no one, it moves to another spot 5 m further on toward
  the other side, up to four times a round. After that, or with 20 seconds left, it goes hunting.
- **Never re-peeks the same angle from the same spot.** It remembers every place it's been shot from and which way the
  shot came. It never peeks that angle again from within 2 m of that spot: not a lean out of its cover, not a step out,
  not a held angle. Shot while holding a spot (unless it's nearly finished you), it's straight back in behind its cover
  and comes at you from another angle.

- **Fights.** It strafes side to side, holds still behind low cover, backs off if its gun wants more range, or pushes
  in. In the open with low cover a few steps off that still has you in sight, it goes over to it and fights from
  behind it. Out from a peek or from cover onto you, it stays leaned out round it and fights from there for a second or
  two rather than stepping out into the open.
- **Breaks standoffs.** Able to see you but with only the cover you're behind to shoot at, after 2 to 3.5 seconds it
  goes round to you (more often) or straight in, rather than the two of you sitting there.
- **Gets out of a beating.** Taking 45 or more inside a second and a half, it breaks off at a sprint to cover out of
  their sight and fights on from there, rather than standing in it. Hit in cover by someone it can't see from there,
  it swings out at them straight away; and with nobody about it holds a corner 3.5 seconds at most before going on.
- **Goes where the fight is** (Deathmatch and Team deathmatch). With nobody in mind it heads for wherever shots were
  fired in the last 8 seconds, at a run, rather than wandering; it backs off to heal only under 22 health (and only
  till 55); it sprints more of the time; and it always shoots back at someone it can see.
- **Doesn't sit and wait to be found.** In Showdown a bot that's badly hurt lies low for only 3.5 to 6 seconds before
  it gets on with it, carefully; and a side that's outnumbered no longer digs in to wait, it goes on, quieter and more
  often round the side. So the last one standing isn't a hunt for someone stood in a corner.
- **Hardly stops walking.** On its way somewhere it pauses only now and then (6% of its turns, a quarter to half a
  second, never at a sprint); it used to stop at about one corner in five.
- **Walks about once a round's won**, at a walk, a few steps at a time round where it stands, reloading, guns quiet,
  as you can, instead of standing frozen till the next round.
- **Runs with its melee weapon out** to get somewhere fast (a long way to go, nobody known close, as you would: it's
  lighter than any gun), its gun back out as it gets there, someone comes into sight or it nears where someone was.
- **Walks like a person.** Its way's smoothed into straight runs between corners, each point a little off the middle
  of its cell, and it's already turning toward the next as it comes up on one, rather than walking the grid's lines and
  pivoting on the spot; it eases off as it arrives, each bot has a pace of its own (a few percent either way), it never
  circles back round a point it's gone past, and its body swings round onto a new heading over a moment.
- **Plays by your rules.** No bot fires at a sprint (nobody can in the arena, Run and gun or not), and none sprints with
  the LMG in its hands (you can't either); it moves at
  your pace: slowed down the sights by its gun as you are (86% with a handgun down to 58% with the hunting rifle, a
  magnified scope less again), and by what its gun weighs, as you are. Between rounds, while everyone's picking and
  counting in, it stands still where it spawned, looking where it faces, and doesn't swap or reload.
- **One at a time.** With two or more of you in sight at once (and none right on top of it), it gets out of sight of
  all of you, then peeks from a spot that sees only one. If there's no such spot it goes round another way.
- **Shot at from somewhere it can't see**, standing where that spot can see it, it gets into cover from it first, then
  peeks at it or flanks it.
- **Takes cover** when it's hurt or empty with you close, sprinting to a spot out of sight of everyone it knows of.
- **Falls back to heal.** Under 45 health (40 in Showdown) it breaks off, gets well out of sight and stays there,
  reloading, its gun on where you were. It doesn't peek or go looking till it's healed (88 or more), and only fires
  back if you're right on it or you're looking straight at it. In Showdown, where health doesn't come back, it plays
  safe for the rest of the round unless its side pushes, but it always fights back at whoever it can see (it used to
  hold fire and just sit there being peeked).
- **Confident.** Somewhere it knows someone is (seen or heard in the last 4 seconds) and healthy, it's much likelier
  to go straight at them, at a run while they're more than 6 m off, than to hang back. In a fight it pushes in more.
- **Tunnel vision.** Now and then (about a third of the time) once it's on someone it stays on them whatever else
  turns up, even taking hits from someone else for it.
- **In cover** with nobody in sight it keeps its gun tucked in, low, so nothing of it shows past what it's behind.
- **Swings back out** from cover to a spot that can see where its target was, and fights again.
- **Flanks or pushes** when it loses sight of someone. It either heads straight for the last place it saw them, or
  circles round to a spot off to their side, keeping out of sight of that place on the way. The more of you it knows
  of and the more hurt it is, the likelier it flanks.
- **Searches.** Getting to where you were and finding nobody, it looks round about that spot, out of its own sight,
  rather than giving up on you. Someone who's shot at it it searches for twice before it moves on.
- **Hunts** up its lane when it knows of nobody: north, middle or south, picked each round (each life in the
  respawn modes), so its route changes, keeping to that lane (most of where it heads is in it) rather than drifting
  into the middle with the rest. In the team modes a side's bots spread over the lanes as a team does (as likely as
  they're team players: nearly always veterans, seldom recruits), each its own lane where there are enough of them,
  steering clear of the lanes they've gone down in. Its route there is its own too: each bot each round weighs every
  step of the yard a little differently, so two going the same way, or one round to round, don't trace the same
  line. Some rounds (two in five) it works the yard's edges, round the outside of everyone.
- **Looks about** standing still: the way that's most open (furthest to the first wall), never at the wall in front of
  it, a new way every 2 to 4 seconds.
- **Leans** out round its cover to peek or hold an angle, to whichever side sees round it, and stays out a second or
  two (eased out and back) rather than leaning and snapping straight back.
- **Aims somewhere on you**, picked for each new target and each burst: your head (likelier the better it is), now and
  then your legs, otherwise your middle, and the hits land mostly there. It looks up and down to where it's aiming
  (and a little down ahead of it otherwise), and its upper body tips with its look.
- **Fires from the hip** within 6 m (9 m with a shotgun), a little looser than aimed.
- **Goes for the flag** in Showdown once it's up, to take it or to stop you taking it, and holds it, fighting whoever
  comes. With the other side alone on it (taking it), every bot drops what it's doing and sprints flat out to it, not
  stopping for cover, and goes straight at whoever's on it.
- **Watched from its own eyes:** when you're down in first person and watching a bot teammate, the view is its eyes,
  set where your own eye sits on a body (not out in front of its face), as wide as your own view, looking where it
  looks (up and down too), leaning as it leans. Its arms and gun are shown exactly as your own first-person view shows
  yours for that weapon: the same hold, offset right and low, for every gun (handguns, machine pistols, sub guns,
  rifles, the battle and hunting rifles, the machine gun, every shotgun including the auto shotgun) and its melee weapon,
  recorded from your own view in the game for each weapon from the hip, down the sights, at a sprint and mid-reload. It
  eases between them as the bot aims, sprints and reloads, breathes and bobs with its stride, and a weapon it switches
  to comes up into view from below. Down the sights the sight it actually has (an optic or its iron sights) is brought
  onto the middle of the view, and the view draws in as yours does; sights' glass is clear to look through. Each shot
  kicks the gun up and back in its hands (as your own does, a shotgun hardest), the hands carried with it; a pump's
  support hand rides the forend back and forward as it's racked, and a bolt gun's trigger hand goes up to the knob and
  works the bolt. There's no teammate outline round it. The view moves as yours would: each of its shots kicks it up,
  each hit it takes makes it flinch, and its stride bobs it. Watching a player, their own view already has all that
  (their arms and gun as their view sent them); each of their shots also kicks the gun in their hands, and a hit they
  take flinches the view.
- **Seen from outside**, a bot's support hand rides its pump's forend as it's racked and its trigger hand works its
  bolt, and anyone's gun flips up about the grip with each shot as well as kicking back.
- **Pumps and bolts worked as yours are.** Someone else's pump or bolt (watched from their eyes or seen from outside)
  is worked with your own cycle's beats and curves (weapons/casings.js remoteCycle): a beat of wind-up, the yank back
  quickest as it hits the stop, a bounce off it, the slam home and a bounce off the front, a bolt's handle snapped up
  and slapped down. Each shot picks one of the same few ways yours are worked. The hand on a bolt's knob goes there and back with it, and
  the case flies out as the action hits its stop. It used to be one plain back-and-forth curve that looked stiff
  next to your own.
- **A bot looking its gun over** turns it about where its hand grips it (with the gun's own grip point,
  player/hold.js), so the gun stays in its hand; it used to turn about a point well below the hand and swing out to
  the side, floating free.
- **Switching who you watch** (your side's all down and it moves to the other side, or a teammate you were watching
  goes down) is a cut: the view dips to dark for a third of a second and comes up on them, rather than whipping across
  in one hard flick. A bot's view is eased more gently than a person's (it sends its aim less often), so it doesn't
  snap from one sent aim to the next.
- **The poses were recorded again** after the first set caught the long guns, the sub guns and the hand cannon
  mid-draw, still slung on the back, so from a bot's eyes the gun sat off the bottom of the view; now every gun is held
  from the hip and down the sights exactly as your own is.
- **No stall as a round starts.** A Showdown round's new loadouts used to build every bot's guns in the very frame the
  round began (seconds on a slow machine); now each survivor's guns are built a few milliseconds' worth at a time over
  the next frames, the one you're watching and anyone in view first, so the round starts smoothly.

**Its side plans together** (the team modes, twice a second):

- **Rushes.** In Showdown, with more of them up than of you and someone known, they sometimes (a bit over half the
  time, each time the numbers change) all rush that spot together at a run, firing as they go and not stopping for
  cover unless nearly dead. It lasts up to 12 seconds, or till the numbers even up.
- **Holds.** Outnumbered, they sit back in cover where they are and wait, peeking now and then.
- **Plays the clock.** In the last 15 seconds of a Showdown round, the side that would lose on time (fewer up, or less
  health between them) pushes; the side ahead hides and waits it out. Once the flag's up they all go for it.

**It learns** over a Showdown match: the lane it went down in, it takes less often in the next rounds (and the lane it
lived through a little more often), and going down in the first 20 seconds of a round on the attack makes it more
careful (less keen to push and to rush), while making it through a round makes it a little bolder. When one goes down,
its side's other bots (as team players) know roughly where the shot came from.

Each class fights its own way: the Marksman hangs back at range and swaps to the revolver up close; the Battle
rifleman holds the long middle distances with the SCAR, the hand cannon up close; the Breacher, the Rusher and the
Enforcer press in with the shotgun; the Scout flanks and fights close with its quiet SMG; the Rifleman and the Gunner
hold the middle distances.

Its aim isn't perfect, and it misses. It takes a moment to react to someone new, its aim starts off them and settles
onto them, and its shots spread more:

- on the move (a little), and a lot at a sprint;
- the longer it holds the trigger, faster with a gun that kicks harder;
- the harder its gun kicks at all;
- the further off you are (past 12 m), and the faster you're moving;
- with no sight a touch more than with a red dot or holo; a magnified scope makes it miss less at range and more up
  close (the stronger the scope the more so), blending from one to the other between 8 and 20 m.

It turns like a hand on a mouse, speeding up toward a target and easing off as it gets there, never snapping onto
you: no faster than 2.1 to 3.2 radians a second (recruit to veteran) at the most, and gently onto it. Off a fight what it wants to look at is eased onto too, so it never whips round and back: a sound it hears it
turns to only if it hasn't just turned to another (one every 1.2 seconds; a hit at once), and where it's walking it
looks a little way on along its path rather than wherever each step takes it. It sticks with whoever it's fighting
rather than flicking between two. It fires in bursts at range, longer ones close in, and single aimed shots from the
rest.

**Changing class.** A bot keeps a record of kills and deaths with each class. Once it has died two more times than
it's killed with one, it switches, picking one that answers how it keeps dying: the long-range classes when it's
being shot from more than 16 m, the close-range ones when it's dying within 7 m.

Its body moves like a player's. The poses were recorded from a survivor in this game, not drawn by hand:

- its stride for walking eight ways against where it aims, and for sprinting;
- its arms and gun aimed, from the hip, or down at a sprint, for each class's guns.

**Nothing of the farm** shows in the arena: no burn barrel fire or moths round its lamps. Running a gun dry says
**Empty. Switch weapons** with no "craft some at the workbench" popup, as there's no workbench.

**Close buttons** are the same plain × on every menu (dim, lighting up on hover), with no box or outline; the settings
menu used to have two of them.

**The pause screen** has no close cross: Resume (or Esc) takes you back. The host's has **End game** (see How a run
goes), which ends the match for everyone and goes to the results.

**No stall at a new round.** When the next round brings a new random loadout, it's drawn at the end of the round
before and readied a piece at a time over the four seconds the round's end lasts: the loadout card's pictures, every
other player's guns as they'll carry them, and their shaders. So the teleport back to the spawns no longer freezes the
game while all of it is built at once. That building waits about two seconds into the round's end, so it doesn't land
while the score bar's shine is playing.

**The score bar's shine** at a round's end (the winning side lit, a sweep across it, its number and pip popping) moves
only things the graphics card can move on its own (fading and sliding), no glow or shadow redrawn each frame, so it no
longer stutters the game as it plays.

**A version check.** A game that's a build behind or ahead of the host's (a page not refreshed since an update) is
turned away when it tries to join, with a note to refresh. The two would otherwise play different games: a joiner on
an old page could find themselves on the farm while everyone else was in the arena.

**Smoother for a joiner.** Everyone's pose (where they are and how they stand, their gun, sent 15 times a second for
each player and each bot) now goes by a channel of its own that never waits: unordered, and never sent again if a
packet's lost, since the next one's along in a fifteenth of a second anyway. It used to share the one reliable,
in-order connection with everything else, so a single dropped packet held back every message behind it until it had
been sent again, and a joiner saw everyone freeze and then jump. A pose that comes in after a newer one is dropped.
Everything that has to arrive (shots, hits, kills, chat, the score) still goes the reliable way, and so does a pose too
big for one packet. Between tabs on one computer (the local test mode) nothing changes.

**Lighter survivors.** A speed loader and a handful of shells are only on someone's rig while they hold them; they
used to sit on every survivor, hidden, with the speed loader's two hundred pieces walked over every frame for each.
The station labels from the farm's first prep no longer work out their places every frame in the arena, where there are
no stations.

A match opens on the **class picker**: five cards, each with its two guns and its melee weapon (each pictured) and
its bandages. Click one or press 1 to 5. Everyone stands frozen at their spawn while picking (5 seconds at the start, 4
between rounds, in every mode). Clicking a card never takes the mouse for the game; it's taken back when the picker closes. The picker
sits under the pause screen, never over it. At the start, the picker's clock waits until every player has loaded into the arena (up to 15
seconds), saying so while it waits.

Then comes the **count-in**, before every match and every Showdown round:

- A big number counts down from 5 (from 3 in Showdown) just above the crosshair, so it never covers what you are
  aiming at. Each one pops in large and settles, then shrinks away as its second runs, with a tick.
- The screen is grey through it until 2, then fades back to full colour over the last two seconds.
- Through the picker and the count-in you can't walk, shoot, swap weapons, reload, lean or aim down the sights (aim
  held as it goes live takes you down them); you can look around, change class, and in the count-in (not while the
  loadout's shown) look your gun over. The bots keep the same rules. A new round straightens you up out of any lean.
- Once a round's been won, till the next starts, you can't shoot, but you can walk about, reload, lean and aim (and
  the bots walk about too); only on the match's results at the very end are you held where you stand; a pump's or a
  bolt gun's action still gets worked. Your survivor stands straight at the spawn from the first moment (it used to ease up out of the title
  screen's kneel, the view rising from about your knees).
- At 0 it calls **Fight**.
- **Match point and the final round** are called out in Showdown: the count-in shows **Match point** (gold) or
  **Final round** (red) big over the number with a low horn, and the score bar's "First to" line becomes the same word,
  pulsing, for the whole round.

The classes went from five to eight (the Battle rifleman, the Scout and the Enforcer are new), each with a job of its
own, and every class carries more of its attachments and a mod than it did. The bots take any of them. Press 1 to 8 on
the picker.

**Loadouts.** The title screen's **Loadouts** button opens a gunsmith for the eight classes (ui/arena-loadouts.js),
in four plain groups: the points along the top, the classes down the left, the weapon and its attachments in the
middle, and the weapons for the slot (with the class's perks) down the right. Words are set in Archivo, as on the
co-op screen, at sizes that read; names and numbers in the game's condensed face. The attachments sit under a heading
that says how many of its ten points that gun is using, and scroll in their own space in a small window (they never
run over the foot). It is laid out like a shooter's: the classes down the left (1 to 8, or the up and down arrows), the slot tabs along the top
(**Primary**, **Secondary**, **Melee**; Q and E step through them), the weapon itself in the middle, turning slowly in
the same studio as the Character menu's (drag to turn it, scroll to zoom), with its arena damage, fire
rate, capacity and reload under it (what the attachments add in green, what they cost in red), and a tile for each
attachment slot it takes under that. Down the right, every weapon that can go in the slot, each pictured, and the
class's perks, which stay as the class has them. The weapon lists are grouped under headings by kind (assault rifles, submachine guns, shotguns, DMRs, bolt action,
light machine guns, handguns; a secondary's list puts the handguns first). The weapon in the middle turns slowly and can be
turned with a drag and zoomed with the wheel, but not moved about (the right or middle button does nothing there). The
points meter holds what something would cost while the pointer moves from one row to the next, rather than blinking off
and on between them.

- **Ten points a gun.** Each gun has 10 points of its own for what goes on it (the two used to share 10 between them,
  then had 6 each).
  The guns themselves are free, and so is the melee weapon, with one exception: a primary's kind of gun (a long gun or
  an automatic, such as the Rusher's double barrel) carried as the secondary takes 2 of that gun's 10 points; a handgun
  there is free. Each step of an attachment costs 1 (a scope at step 3 costs 3, a heavy-duty suppressor 3), a
  conversion kit 1 a step, the pump's pair loading 1 and triple loading 2; a kind of shell is free (each is a
  sidegrade). The meter along the top shows the ten pips of the gun on show (**Primary points** or **Secondary
  points**; the primary's on the melee tab): what's fitted lit orange, and the 2 for carrying a primary's kind as the
  secondary pale, first. Hovering anything shows what it would add (striped in) or give back (hollowed out). Anything
  that would take that gun past 10 is dimmed and can't be picked; trying anyway shakes the meter. The number by each
  class in the list is both its guns' points added together.
- **Attachments.** Click a tile for its next step, click a pip for that exact step (its own pip again takes that step
  off), and the cross or a right click takes it off. Only real attachments for that gun, as far as each goes on it
  (the workbench's own limits): sights up to the gun's strongest scope (the LMG's the ACOG), a can, a choke on the
  shotguns (it shares the muzzle with the can: fitting one takes the other off), laser, light, the speed loader where
  there is one, the trigger, the magazine (not on the LMG: in the arena it keeps its 100-round belt, no longer one). Never the powder or a tune-up.
- **The gun's own parts.** Every part the workbench can fit to that one gun has its own tile too, each named for the
  part, one point a level: the handgun's **Barrel** (match barrel I to III) and **Stock**; the revolver's **Barrel**
  (4", 6" or 8") and **Hammer** (fan the hammer), and **Stock**; the hand cannon's **Compensator** (I to III) and
  **Stock**; the pump's **Action** (slam fire I to III); the double barrel's **Trigger** (the twin trigger, one
  step); the hunting rifle's **Bolt** (greased); the machine pistol's ported **Barrel** (I to III); the SMG's **Trigger
  group** (navy burst, I to III); the M4's **Mag well** (flared, I to III) and **Barrel** (heavy, I to III); the LMG's
  **Bipod** (I to III). A sidearm's folding shoulder stock costs 2: 45% less kick, fast shots kept tighter and the gun
  steadier in the hands. A compensator or a ported barrel takes the muzzle, so fitting one takes off a can or a choke,
  and the other way round, with the points given back. The grenade launcher and the auto sear's own tile aren't
  offered (the auto sear stays on the Conversion tile). They go on as the workbench's do: everyone else sees them on
  your gun, and the stock classes' own (the Rifleman's mag well, the Marksman's 4" barrel, the Battle rifleman's
  compensator, the Scout's match barrel, the Breacher's slam fire, the Enforcer's hammer, the Gunner's bipod) show on
  their tiles and are counted. Nothing hits harder in the arena (damage is capped at the gun's own), so a barrel's
  worth is in its spread and how far it keeps its damage.
- **What each part costs you.** A part that only helped now has a drawback too, here and at the workbench, shown on
  its card under Costs:
  - the **match barrel**'s heavier slide is 8% slower to bring up to the eye (3% more a level);
  - the revolver's **longer barrels** weigh it down (2% off your pace, 1.5% more a level) and are 8% slower up to
    the eye (5% more a level);
  - the hand cannon's **compensator** is 6% slower up to the eye;
  - the machine pistol's **ported barrel** bleeds gas, so its rounds lose damage sooner (its drop-off 12% closer, 5%
    more a level);
  - the M4's **heavy barrel** weighs it down (3% off your pace, 1% more a level) and is 8% slower up to the eye (4%
    more a level), about 10% slower all told at level I;
  - the LMG's **bipod** legs weigh it down (3% off your pace, 1% more a level) and make it 10% slower up to the eye;
  - the **greased bolt** locks up a little looser: 10% more spread;
  - a sidearm's **shoulder stock** has to be shouldered first: 10% slower up to the eye.
  The rest already had one (slam fire's spread, fanning's spread, the navy group's burst in place of full auto, the
  twin trigger's two barrels at once); the flared mag well's small saving on a reload is left as it was.
- **The heavy barrel** (the M4's, at the workbench as well) now keeps its damage further out as well as hitting
  harder and spreading less (and weighs more, above): its drop-off starts and bottoms out 30% further away, and each level after the first
  another 20%. In the arena that's about 11 m more range at full strength, with 30% less spread.
- **Conversion kits.** A gun that has one gets a **Conversion** tile, one kit at a time, picked off a row of choices
  with **Stock** first (the cross or a right click puts it back): the handgun's burst conversion (I or II, 1 or 2
  points), full auto (the auto sear, the third step of that line: 3) or a second pistol (1); a second machine pistol
  (1); the submachine gun's 10mm kit (1); the double barrel's sawn-off (one step, 1); the battle rifle's AK kit
  (1). They go on as the workbench's do, levels and all. A kit that takes a line's place (the AK kit the magazine and
  magwell, the 10mm kit the magazine) shuts that tile while it's on, and gives back what was on it.
- **Shells and loading.** A shotgun's **Shells** tile picks what it starts loaded with: buckshot, slugs, flechettes
  or dragon's breath, all free. Every shotgun in the arena carries all four kinds, so in a match X swaps to the next
  kind with a reload, as it does at the farm (see **Fire** below for dragon's breath on people). A slug hits as 60%
  of a whole load of buckshot, in one, and carries far further. The pump's **Loading** tile picks how many shells go in
  a go: one at a time (stock), pair loading (1 point) or triple loading (2), as Quick hands II and III load it (a class
  whose perks already load it faster keeps the faster). The double barrel's Sleight of Hand stays its loader tile.
- **Pros and cons.** Hover (or tab onto) any pip, tile or choice and a card over it says what that step or option does, in
  two columns, **Gives** and **Costs**, a line each,
  worked out from the gun with it against the gun without it: damage against a body (the arena's own), pellets, fire
  rate, hip and aimed spread, recoil, rounds, reload, ADS speed, move speed and range, green where it helps and red
  where it hurts, with what numbers can't say written in (muffled shots, zoom, bursts, full auto, no aiming down the
  sights with a gun in each hand, a light for the night, fire that burns out past 8 m).
- **Names.** Rename a class with its **Rename** button (on the row under the pointer), a double click on its name, or
  F2: Enter or clicking away keeps it, Esc leaves it as it was, up to 18 characters, and an empty name puts its own back.
  Your name for it is on the list, the class picker's cards, the class shown down the left in the match and its toasts;
  the bots and everyone else's classes keep the stock names. **Reset to default** puts the name back too.
- **Guns.** A primary is any long gun or automatic; the secondary a sidearm or any gun that rides in a different slot
  from the primary (the Rusher's double barrel behind an SMG), or **None** to carry no secondary. A new gun starts
  bare. Picking a primary that rides where the secondary does takes the secondary off.
- **Kept.** Every change is saved at once, per class, in this browser. A class you haven't touched carries its stock
  kit, mods and all (a stock conversion, the Scout's 10mm kit or the Rusher's sawn-off, shows on its tile and is
  counted, as are its own parts'); change anything and it's yours, all of that kept (the note under the perks says
  which it is). A
  dot by a class in the list marks one you've changed; **Reset to default** (or R) puts it back. Every stock class
  already fits in 10 points a gun (1 to 6 on each, its conversion, its own parts and the secondary's 2 counted), so
  none is trimmed. A kit carrying the LMG gets one spare belt, as the Gunner
  does; anything else three mags.
- **In the match.** Pick the class as ever and you carry your version of it, solo or online; the class picker's cards
  show it. Everyone else sees your guns as they are on you, attachments and all. The bots always carry the stock
  classes, and Showdown's random loadouts are everyone's whatever you've made.

**Blood on you** is wiped clean at once when you come back in, at the start of each Showdown round and at the end of a
match (it used to stay on your survivor and your weapons between lives, rounds and games); everyone else's is wiped
when they come back in, too.

**The damage card** lists everyone else who hurt you that life, each on its own row in their colour with a bar of
what they did and how many hits: an assist (30 or more) is picked out with an **Assist** tag and its row in their
colour.

**Changing class mid-match.** Press **L** to bring the class picker up over the match, and again to put it away. A
class picked while your spawn protection's still running (you've only just come in and haven't fired) is yours at
once; otherwise it's yours the next time you come in. While you're down the picker only comes up if you press L or F: the
damage card says so, under when you'll be back, with Space to skip the death camera.

**Stamina** is fairer in the arena (the bots never tire): a sprint burns about two thirds of what it does on the farm
(about 11 seconds of running from full), it starts coming back after a third of the usual breather and more than
twice as fast, and running it dry never locks you out: as soon as there's any back you can sprint on it.

**No bandages, no bleeding.** Your health comes back on its own:

- It starts once you've gone 3.75 seconds without being hurt.
- It comes back slowly at first, then faster and faster.
- However low you were, you're full again 8.5 seconds after the last hit.
- Once it's started, the health bar shows all that's coming back as a blue stretch ahead of what you have.
- Not in Showdown: there nothing comes back.

Getting hurt again starts the wait over. Bots heal the same way.

The perks are the survival game's skills and work the same way:

- **Steady aim**: every shot lands closer to the crosshair.
- **Quick hands**: quicker reloads. **Quick hands II** is quicker again, and the pump takes two shells a go.
- **Iron lungs**: more stamina, which comes back sooner.
- **Good boots**: faster walking and sprinting.
- **Run and gun II**: SMGs, pistols, shotguns and the assault rifle fire while you sprint, with a lot more spread.
- **Vault fire**: quicker vaults, and you can shoot while going over.
- **Quick draw**: quicker weapon swaps.

Each card pictures its guns with the class's own attachments and mods, whatever's fitted to yours. A can adds its little damage between players too, as it does against zombies. You only ever have your class's perks; picking another class swaps them. Each card in the picker lists the mods (the sawn-off's
Sleight of Hand and the like) and the attachments under each gun, and the perks underneath. A gun's mod parts are drawn
in gold in its picture, the sawn-off's cut-down grip with them.

Each gun starts with a full mag and three more (the LMG one more box; shotguns at least 14 shells). There are no
pickups, and coming back in gives you a fresh loadout.

**Damage between players** is its own table rather than the zombies', so no gun kills everything in one shot. The
table was balanced as a whole (the automatics brought closer together, the handgun and the hunting rifle up a little),
and every gun hits with its full weight (a pass that had eased everything down to 80% was taken back: the guns felt
like pea-shooters). To a 100-health body, before range falloff:

|---|---|---|---|
| Handgun | 26 | 4 | 0.63 s |
| Revolver | 46 | 3 | 0.84 s |
| Hand cannon | 58 | 2 | 0.34 s |
| Machine pistol | 15 | 7 | 0.45 s |
| Submachine gun | 18 | 6 | 0.36 s |
| Assault rifle | 22 | 5 | 0.38 s |
| Light machine gun | 19 | 6 | 0.35 s |

- **Where it lands.** A head takes half again a body's damage (1.5×: heads are small, so a headshot is worth more but
  not twice as much), and an arm or leg about three quarters. The marksman's guns, the hunting rifle and the battle
  rifle, do 1.8× through the head (down from double: still the most of any gun, a hunting rifle still kills with one). A shotgun pellet in the head is 1.2× up close, less the further off, down to no more than a body pellet by where its damage bottoms out, so a few
  stray pellets in the head from far off never nearly kill.
- **Your head where it is.** A head leaned out round a corner is hit where it is, not over the middle of the body
  behind the cover: with your crosshair on it from your own eyes, it's a headshot.
- **Where the reticle is, the round goes.** From your own eyes a shot leaves from your eye where it is as the view's
  drawn (down the sights, on the sight's own line), not from the head above it, so there's no height over bore: the
  reticle or the front post on someone's head is a headshot at any range, and just over their head is a miss. What a
  round hits is whatever part of their body it actually goes into (their head, chest, arms or legs as they stand,
  crouch or lean), not a band of height off the ground (those were set for a taller figure: a round at the middle of
  the head went into the chest, and one just over the top of it counted as a headshot).
- **Nothing fitted hits harder here.** A burst conversion, an auto sear, a match barrel or any other mod that adds
  damage against zombies adds none between players, for the balance (anything that lowers it still does).
- **Fire modes.** A gun that can fire full auto or in bursts starts on the fullest it has (full auto over a burst over
  single shots), rather than what it does out of the box.
- **Shotguns.** Each pellet counts: the pump's eight are 13 apiece and the double's 12 (the sawn-off's more, and
  harder up close), the auto shotgun's seven 14 apiece (98 all told up close: a solid two-shot kill at
  close range, it used to feel weak there). Between players their damage falls off far harder than against
  zombies: the pump from 4 m to a quarter of it by 11 m, the auto shotgun from 5 m to about a quarter by 12 m, the double barrel from 3 m to a
  fifth by 10 m (a slug keeps its own drop-off). In the arena a shotgun's **pattern is fixed**, as competitive
  shooters have it: one pellet on the crosshair and the rest on two rings round it (every other one on the inner),
  only turned a little shell to shell, so a shell up close does what it did last time rather than a lucky or an
  unlucky spread (from above, the same spread across).
- **The hand cannon** keeps its full damage out to 14 m and still 72% of it at 32 m (the zombie game's drop to a
  third past a few metres left it doing next to nothing at range).
- **Melee** hits for 38 (bat) to 92 (sledgehammer). A blow to the head does 1.25×, and so does an axe's chop more often.
- **Who decides a hit.** Whoever fires works out the hit against where the target stands on their screen, and the
  target's game takes the damage.
- **Spawn protection.** For 1 second after you come in, nothing hurts you. Firing, swinging or shoving ends it at
  once. A strip under the crosshair runs down while it lasts.
- **Shoves** (V) work on other players and bots too, in front of you and within reach. They're knocked back, can't
  move or fire for about half a second, and take a little damage. Their view jolts and blurs for a second, easing off
  as it goes.

**Hit feedback.**

- **Damage numbers** pop up just over the head of whoever you hit, from either view, and add up while you keep hitting
  them, growing as they do (20 px up to 36). They're white, and turn gold once a hit's gone in the head; a hit of 40 or
  more pops in harder, with a twist. Near where you're aiming (within about 45 px, easing back by 150) they fade to a
  fifth of themselves, so they never sit over what you're shooting at.
- **On them:** whoever you hit flashes a white outline for a sixth of a second (gold through the head), even someone
  on the other side who otherwise has none, and their upper body jolts the way the round went (their head snaps with
  it on a headshot), easing back.
- **The sound:** a crisp, dry tick over the wet sound of it going in, with a thump through their body under it,
  heavier the harder it hit, and a bright ping on top through the head.
- **Tagging.** A hit slows whoever takes it hard, as in Counter-Strike but more so: any hit takes you down to about
  half pace (a 22-damage rifle hit to about 45%), a heavy one to about a third, and breaks a sprint; it wears off over
  a second and a half to two. Bots are slowed the same way.
- Each hit makes the wet sound of it going in, a duller one to the head, and a burst head pops.
- Down the sights, where the crosshair's put away, a hit shows the crosshair's own hitmarker over the middle of the
  sight: the same four strokes on the diagonals, the same size and the same quick pop and fade as from the hip, white for
  a hit, gold for the head, red and bigger for a kill. It's drawn the same way as the hip one (the same strokes and
  the same eased pop), so down the sights it's as smooth as from the hip.
- **The hitmarker shows where you hit.** From the hip or down the sights, the marker sits over the point the round
  struck (worked out from where it landed on them, seen from your own eyes), kept inside the crosshair's circle, so a
  hit high on the shoulder shows up and right of the middle, a leg low; it holds there for its pop and fade.

**Cover and sight.**

- From your own eyes, a round fired below the top of a barrier stops on it, so someone crouched in behind one shows
  only their head and shoulders.
- From above, a hit on someone just behind low cover lands only on their head or an arm.
- From above, the fog of war hides anyone on the other side you can't see, their name and all, until they come into
  sight.
- Nobody on the other side is outlined or shown through walls, from either view.
- Their name shows only while you're aiming at them or looking right at them, in plain sight. From your own eyes
  that means near the middle of the view (more leeway close up); from above, your crosshair on the ground close to
  them. It shows in red, a little see-through, never with their health, and never pinned to the edge of the screen.
  Your own side keeps its outlines, names and health bars in its colour, and your teammates are shown through walls
  while they're alive, gone the moment they go down: from your own eyes as the thin line round them that the last
  zombies of a wave get (just their outline, not their gun or pack filled in through the wall), from above as a
  see-through shape. The line shows however close you're standing to the wall between you (anything within 1.2 m of
  your eyes used to hide it, so up against cover it went).
- **Spawns** in Deathmatch and when you come back in a team game go far from everyone on the other side, and never to
  a spot any of them can see from where they stand, if there's any other choice; not always the very furthest, so
  they can't be learned: any unseen spot at least three quarters as far as the furthest, picked at random.

**Gore.**

- Every hit throws blood off where it struck, on everyone's screen, and drips on the ground behind.
- **Blood on your screen** when it's close: shoot someone within a few metres (the closer and the harder, the more), cut
  them with a blade, or burst a head right in front of you, and blood spatters your screen as zombie gore does.
- **Where it comes from.** The blood comes in from where the hit was against where you're facing: someone shot in
  front of you sprays the middle of the screen and out across most of it; shot off to your left or right, it comes
  from that side of the screen, as if it flew in from there; anyone hit behind you puts nothing on your screen. Each
  drop flies in from off its side and lands with a small splat (a quick swell and settle), the haze rising in behind.
- When you're hit, the screen bloods from that side, your survivor stains, you flinch, and the hit indicator points
  at the shooter.
- **Watching someone from their eyes** (down, in first person), their screen's blood is yours: a hit they take bloods
  it from the shooter's side, and anyone they shoot or cut close up, or a head bursting near them, spatters it from
  where it happened against the way they're looking.
- A hit puts a fine red mist in the air where it went in, and blood out of their back the way the round was going:
  a spray behind them and drops splashing on the floor a metre or two back (more for a heavy round or the head).
- A kill leaves a pool spreading under the body.
- **Blood on them.** Everyone carries blood as much as they're hurt: a little after a hit or two, soaked near death
  (your own survivor too). It stays while their health does, so in Showdown it's there till the round ends, and in
  the respawn modes it fades off as their health comes back.
- A blade to the head on the killing blow splits it in two, as it does a zombie's.
- A headshot kill that hits hard enough (60 or more) bursts the head in a red mist with chips of skull, on every
  screen, and the head stays gone until that player is back.
- **Limbs come off** as a zombie's do, on the hit that kills:
  - A shotgun within 7 m blows the arm or leg it hit (or one at random) to red mist.
  - A blade, or a heavy round (hunting rifle, revolver, hand cannon) to an arm or a leg, throws it off whole, tumbling
    away.
  - Blood pumps from where it was for a couple of seconds, and the player's whole again when they're back.

**The HUD.**

- **The kill feed** runs down the middle of the left side of the screen. Each line shows the killer and, after a +,

  each death), then the whole table: names in a plain readable face, numbers in the game's condensed one, each side
  under its own coloured heading with its score, your row lit and the MVP tagged. The buttons sit at the foot on the

  **Lobby** goes back to the lobby.
- **Going down.** Nothing crowds the screen: no black bars, no call over the middle. A moment after you fall a card
  comes up near the bottom: who got you (their picture), with what (its picture), a headshot and how far off, the

- **A kill** brings up a marker under the crosshair: its mark swelling in out of a flash, a line drawn out either side
  and the name; kills in quick succession are called (Double kill, Triple kill, Quad kill, Rampage).
- **A teammate down** (the team modes) slides in below the score bar, clear of it, in red, with how many of your side are left.

- **Watching someone** (a teammate, or the one who got you), the banner saying who sits below the score along the top,

- **Teammates' dots**, the dot over their head, are in the same colour as their outline (their own colour), with
  their name in your side's.
- **The other side from above**, shown through cover where you can see them, is outlined in red, whoever they are.
- **Your class and perks** show as a strip just above your health (above your team's list in the team modes): bright
  for a couple of seconds as you come in, then faded back out of the way.
- **The end of the match** slows the game down more and more as the last kill lands, to a dead stop about three
  seconds later (2.8 s). Time holds stopped for a beat (0.6 s), and only then does the scoreboard come up over the
  frozen moment. The clock and the HUD run on real time, so they carry on.
  - Everything slows with it: every sound playing and every new one (pitched down as it slows), the others' bodies
    and guns, tracers, smoke and particles, and your own arms and gun from your own eyes (a swing, a reload, a bolt,
    the recoil and the flinch all wind down with the world).
  - It drops off hard the moment the last kill lands (to about a third speed at once), then winds on down from there,
    so it's plain the match is over.
  - You can still shoot while it slows (at its own slowed pace), and your mouse sensitivity slows with it, down to about
    a fifth as it stops.
  - Looking round while the scoreboard's up, your arms and gun turn with the view (they no longer stay pointed where
    you last aimed).
  - No sound from the results coming up, and none from your loadout going back into your hands as you come back in.
- **Damage numbers** rise straight up and out of whoever you hit, from where they stand, hidden until they're in
  place (they no longer slide in from the side).
- **Killed by a hit to the head**, a round or a blow, or with your head taken off by whatever hit you (a sledgehammer's
  crush, a blade's split, a round that bursts it), it's a beat of its own: a quick red flash, a dark red vignette in
  from the edges that leaves the middle of the view clear, a soft, short ringing in your ears (much quieter than it was, the world's sound only a
  little dulled under it), the world drained of colour and a touch soft, the colour seeping back as the death camera turns to
  whoever did it, all of it gone in about 1.8 seconds. It never blacks out the view.
- **Space** skips the death camera a second after you go down: in the respawn modes you're straight back in (unless
  you've opened the loadouts), in Showdown straight on to watching a teammate. **F** opens the loadouts for your next
  life (the card says both).
- **Lasers in the arena** are a faint pencil of light rather than a searchlight: your own beam at half its usual
  brightness and never more than about 2.5 pixels wide on screen, everyone else's (players' and bots') at under a
  third and 1.5 pixels, and the dot where it lands and the glint at its lens dimmed to match. They used to be drawn
  at least 7 pixels wide at full brightness, so a laser gave its owner away from across the map. Outside the arena
  they're as they were.
- **Changing your loadout while you're down** (the respawn modes) holds you there: the countdown still runs, but at
  0 it waits, and you're only back in, with the new loadout, when you press **Space** (the card says "Back in 3 · then
  [Space]", then "[Space] to go back in"). It used to drop you straight back in the moment the countdown ran out, maybe
  before you'd finished picking.
- **The death camera** goes to whoever killed you and follows them: from above it keeps them in the middle, and from
  your own eyes it draws out of yours and then follows from behind and above them, from your side. It lasts till
  you're back in. In Showdown only once your whole side is down (then it goes to whoever took the last of you down,
  and on to their own eyes); before that you watch your teammates. It stays on them even if they've gone down
  since (a trade), looking at where they lie. The move over to them is one smooth curve from where your fall left the
  view, about a second and a half, lifting a little in the middle, its aim swinging round with it; where they stand
  and where it sits are smoothed, so their steps and the network's corrections never shake it (it used to chase them
  frame by frame, and stuttered). Their crosshair isn't shown (only a teammate's is, when you watch one).
- **Inspecting** your weapon from your own eyes puts the HUD away but leaves the count-in, the score, the kill feed,
  the results and the scoreboard up. You can't inspect while the class picker is up.

  fetched up against: papers and rags turned along it, cardboard flat at its foot, cans and bottles rolled into the
  angle. Bricks and a broken plank lie in one heap at the foot of the odd wall that's crumbled. Spent cases lie only
  behind low cover, where someone fought from it. Tyres are stacked two high in some outdoor corners.
- **Camps** in corners out of the wind: a sleeping bag, a ring of stones round the fire's charred sticks, a pot, a pack,
  empty tins; and a body bag or two laid out by a wall.
- **The walls and everything tall** (fewer of them than before, and the words and marks only on walls and buildings,
  never on a container, a crate or a car): what was sprayed and written as the town emptied (THEY'RE INSIDE, NO WAY OUT,
  GOD HELP US, EVAC CANCELLED, the search teams' X marks with their dates and counts, tallies of the days), bloody
  handprints and smears, bullet holes, missing posters, soot licked up from fires, rust and water stains. Each is laid
  flat on a real face, found by a ray from the floor in front of it, its corners checked to sit on the same face, so
  none hangs off an edge, into a doorway or in the air.
- **The tops of things** (what the camera from above sees most): rust, grime and standing water over the roofs of the
  containers, lorries, buildings and stacks, and now and then what someone left up there: a tarp held down with crates,
  jerry cans, a sleeping bag, sandbags round a lookout, a radio mast and its set.
- **Smoke** rising from fires still burning past the north and west edges (the far side from the camera from above),
  outdoors.
- **What it keeps to:** nothing on the floor stands higher than a boot (the tallest, a sleeping bag or a body bag, about
  a quarter metre), nothing it adds stops you or a round, and what's on top of tall things is already above every line
  of sight. It's the same on every screen (seeded by the map). The floor's sheet is painted on one of the game's
  **worker threads** (an OffscreenCanvas, its picture handed back without a copy: see "The game's worker threads"
  below), so the slowest part never holds up the main thread; where a browser has no workers it's painted in place. The rays that find the walls and the tops are tested only against
  what's near them (the map's pieces sorted into 2 m buckets), so dressing a map takes well under a tenth of a second.
- Signs, posters and painted faces that were folded into a map's merged meshes kept no texture coordinates and came
  out one flat colour (the small maps' shop names, notices and paint); merging now keeps them (core/geometry.js).

**Snipers only, Brawl and Gun game.** Three more arena modes in the lobby, after Showdown. Each is played as a
Deathmatch: everyone for themselves, back in a few seconds after going down.
- **Snipers only.** Everyone carries the same kit: a hunting rifle with its first scope, four loads for it, and a
  machete for anyone who gets close. First to the kill count wins (the lobby's setting, 15 to start).
- **Brawl.** Melee weapons only. You pick yours from seven cards, each showing hits to down someone, swing time,
  reach and pace, with its strength and its weakness. Swap with your loadout key between lives. The bots each pick
  their own, and after a few deaths with one they switch, likelier to something that answers how they died.
- **Gun game.** Everyone climbs the same ladder of twelve weapons, one step per kill: the assault rifle, the battle
  rifle and the LMG, then the two SMGs, the three shotguns, the hand cannon and the handgun. After those come the
  **golden revolver** and the **golden katana**.
  - At the start of each match the host draws every gun's attachments and mods at random: up to five per gun, at any
    tier up to the best. Everyone gets the same draw, and you see it on your card with the ladder under it.
  - Everyone also carries a machete. A machete kill doesn't move you up, but it puts the person you killed back one
    gun.
  - **The golden revolver** holds one round per load, with a dozen spare, and any hit kills. Its tracer is gold.
  - **The golden katana** kills with one blow, any blow, and a kill with it wins the match. If the clock runs out
    first, whoever is furthest up wins.
  - Reaching either golden weapon brings a big gold call over the screen with a fanfare. When anyone else reaches
    one, everyone in the server sees a gold notice across the top of the screen: their name, which golden weapon,
    and how many kills they are from winning. It comes with a chime and fades after about four seconds. Their chip on
    the score bar turns gold too.
- **The golden weapons are gold everywhere**: in your hands, on anyone carrying one, and in every picture of them
  (your slot row, the kill feed, the death card, the loadout card).
- **What a golden weapon hits turns gold.** Mist and drops come out gold instead of blood, and leave no stain on the
  floor, the walls or your view. The pieces they cut off drip gold too.
- **Whoever a golden weapon kills turns to gold**, starting from where the round or blade went in.
  - The gold spreads over them like something poured. It runs downward faster than it climbs, and its front is a
    wobbling line with a molten glow along it.
  - It covers every part of them: clothes, face, hair, and whatever they were holding.
  - The body sets rigid where it stands as the gold starts, with no ragdoll slack. It still takes the shot's force:
    it topples the way it was hit, as one piece, like a statue pushed off its plinth. It goes slowly at first and
    faster as it tips, lands on its side, rocks back once and lies still. A harder hit (a head shot) pushes it over
    faster. They stay a gold statue until they're back in (`goldFallStep` in `effects/gold.js`).
  - The golden katana always takes the head off. Both halves fly off gold, cut faces and all.
  - A chime and a glitter of gold come off the body.
  - Implemented in `effects/gold.js`: each part of the body gets a gold skin of its own shape, drawn over it and showing
    only as far as the pour has reached.

**Fire.** In the arena, dragon's breath sets people alight just as it does zombies (`pvp/pvp-fire.js`):
- **It catches where the pellets went in**, on that limb, and spreads over the rest of them in about a second and a
  half. As it burns they char, the same way a zombie does: black creeps over them in patches with a scorched brown
  edge running ahead, and the char's edge and cracks glow like embers while they're alight. The char stays on them
  until they're back in.
- **It burns for 3 seconds from the last shell that caught them**, 4 damage every half second. That's 24 at most from
  one hit, well under what a zombie takes (7 a tick). Health doesn't come back while you burn.
- **A burn that finishes someone** is the shooter's kill, with the gun that lit them, in the kill feed and the score.
- **The shooter sees each tick** as a smaller orange number over whoever they lit, under their hit numbers.
- **On fire yourself**, the edges of your view burn. Flames lick in from round the edges over a heat shimmer, rising
  as flames do and reaching furthest in along the bottom, deep red at their roots through orange to a yellow core,
  with a warm glow under them. It eases in as the fire takes and fades out as it dies (`HEAT_GLSL` in
  `effects/post.js`). The middle of the screen stays clear.
- **Everyone sees it.** Your game burns you and tells the others, and the host's game burns its bots. Everyone draws
  the flames and the char on whoever's alight.
- **It burns from the moment it catches**: the first tick lands with the hit, and the flames burst up on them at once,
  before the fire spreads.
- **No gore from fire.** A kill by fire never bursts a head or takes a limb off (bullets and blades still do).
- **Bots** only load dragon's breath when their class says so (the Scavenger's pump). It does 80% of buckshot's damage
  and nothing past 8 m. They never take your loadouts as their own.

**A changed loadout says so.** A class whose loadout you've changed on the Loadouts screen says **Custom loadout** in
place of its usual description, on the Loadouts screen and on the class picker (`AL_CUSTOM`, `ui/arena-loadouts.js`).

better). Each is its own trade, so none is simply the best (`PVP_MELEE` in `pvp/pvp.js`):

No two weapons put someone down in the same number of hits both ways. The picker's cards show both: **Hits to down**
and **To the head**. Each weapon's head blow is its own share over the body's (`hm` in `PVP_MELEE`). A swing comes every 1.3 times its swing time, as yours always has. A stagger stops
you moving and swinging for its length, for you and for the bots alike.

**Bots fight hand to hand** (Brawl, the golden katana, and a machete in Gun game).
- They run at you flat out, weaving side to side if you have a gun.
- Close in, they fight like someone with a blade. While their own swing comes back round, they wait just outside your
  reach, or at the edge of theirs if they out-reach you, circling you and changing direction.
- The moment their swing is ready, they step in and swing, stepping in with the blow.
- They get out of a swing they can see coming (yours or another bot's), then come straight back to punish the miss.
- Now and then they feint: in to the edge of reach, then back out to draw your swing.
- Badly hurt against someone healthy, they back off to heal where they can.
- In Gun game, with you right on top of them, they often pull the machete to set you back a gun.
- Each weapon has its own wind-up before the blow lands, from 0.14 s for the crowbar to 0.52 s for the sledgehammer.
  You can see it: their body winds back and whips through the swing, across the body for a slash or down from
  overhead for a chop.

**Melee hits, as on the zombies.** A blow on a person now lands the way it does on a zombie (`meleeBleed` in
`pvp/pvp.js`): a burst of blood driven on along the swing, a mist, a gush out of them, the wall behind and the floor
spattered. A blade's cut throws a line of it off the way the swing went; a blunt blow a heavier burst. Your weapon
takes much more of their blood (a blade's edge the most) and so do you; the farm's zombies leave more on it too.
- **Heard where it lands**: the blade's or the blunt head's sound of going in, a head's wetter, played at the person
  hit for everyone near enough. Every swing is heard from whoever swung it, yours, other players' and the bots'.
- **The sledgehammer takes the head** of whoever it kills, wherever the blow landed, as it does a zombie's: a burst of
  blood and red mist, chips of skull flung off, blood thrown out all round.
- **Everyone holds their melee weapon.** Bots carrying a melee weapon hold it in their hand as a player does (a pose
  for each weapon, recorded off a survivor holding it, at ease and at a sprint), not the pistol's grip.

**Blocking with a melee weapon** (`melee/block.js`). Hold **right click** with a melee weapon out to bring it up
across you as a guard (eased up over about a seventh of a second, the arms bringing it across in front of your face,
the chest turned to put the shoulder behind it).
- **While it's up** you can't swing and you walk at 70%. Holding it costs nothing by itself (no stamina drains while
  it's up, though none comes back either); only the blows it takes cost you. It comes up on any stamina at all, not
  only a full tank.
- **A blow from in front** (within 40° either side of where you face: nothing from the sides or behind) lands on the guard. Only 18% of it gets
  through (45% of a fire axe's or a sledgehammer's, which beat a guard down) and it knocks stamina out of you at once,
  by the weapon: a tenth of the tank for a crowbar, 13% a machete, 15% a katana, 18% a bat, 24% a wrench, a third
  for a fire axe and half for a sledgehammer (a zombie's by how hard it hits). Run out and the guard's **broken**: the
  weapon's knocked down out of your guard, you stagger (the view reels and swims, a cold red vignette and a grey jolt
  of a flash come in from the edges), and it can't come up again for 1.2 s.
- **After a swing** the guard can't come up until the weapon's back across, longer the heavier it is: a crowbar's
  almost at once, 0.38 s after a fire axe's swing, 0.6 s after a sledgehammer's. Bots keep to the same.
- **A parry**: raise the guard within 0.22 s of the blow landing and none of it gets through, it costs nothing (it
  gives you back an eighth of your stamina), and whoever swung is thrown off: staggered and unable to swing for most
  of a second. Parried, your own screen's hit: a cold white flash in from the edges and the view swimming for a moment
  (no words over it).
- **Bullets go straight through** a guard. A zombie's swing lands on it the same way (a brute's harder; a crawler's
  swipe at your ankles goes under it), and a parry sends the zombie reeling.
- **The crosshair** swaps its brackets for a shield (Kenney's Board Game Icons) while the guard's up.
  - It's filled to how much guard you've got left (your stamina): a solid shield when full, the fill draining down
    from the top, only its outline when there's none.
  - Just raised, while a blow would still be parried, it glows gold and stands a little bigger.
  - A parry pops it, white, and throws a ring out off it; a blow on the guard flashes it white and jolts it.
  - Broken, it goes red, splits in two, and its halves tip over and fall away as they fade.
- **What gets through a block bleeds.** The share of a blocked blow that gets through draws a little blood off whoever
  took it, on everyone's screen, as well as the sparks (a parry lets nothing through: sparks only).
- **A blow on the guard** throws sparks off where the steel meets (a white-hot flash and its glow, sparks back toward
  whoever swung and out along the edge; a parry's twice as bright with embers that hang), rings out (a clang through
  your arms; a parry's a bright ring of steel that hangs), and slams the weapon and your fist back at you on the arm's
  springs, knocked aside and down, shaking as it comes back up, with a jolt of the view.
- **Hit mid-swing** (not on a guard), your swing breaks off before it lands, its rush of air cut off short, and your
  next one is held off for about half a second. The same goes for the bots.
- **The bots block too.** Against a swing coming at them they put their guard up (more often the sharper the bot, and
  the more guard it has left), once per swing; a sharper bot times it to parry more often. Their guard wears down as
  it's held and with each blow it takes, breaks the same way, and comes back while it's down. They drop it once the
  blow's come, to hit back. Everyone sees a guard up and its sparks.
- **Only in front.** The guard covers 40° either side of where you face; a blow from the side or behind lands.

**Melee with more to it than block and swing** (`melee/block.js`, the same for the bots).
- **Charged heavy blows.** Keep the button held and the swing stops just before the blow lands and winds up there, the
  weapon trembling back and the crosshair's brackets closing in orange, up to full in about two thirds of a second (a
  rising ring when it's full; it eats a tenth of your stamina a second while held, 2.5 s at most). Let go to bring it
  down: up to 60% harder the longer it was held, and one at full **breaks a guard** outright (and gets 1.6× as much
  through it). Tap it for a quick swing as before. Others see the wind-up and the release on your avatar.
- **Feints.** While it's winding up, raise your guard (right click) to pull the blow: a quick feint that costs a little
  stamina and baits a guard or a parry out of whoever's watching.
- **Ripostes.** Parry a blow and for 0.9 s your next swing comes 28% quicker, hits 40% harder and goes through any guard
  (the crosshair glows gold while it's up).
- **Poise.** A blow landing on you mid-swing breaks your swing off, unless you're past the first 30% of it and swinging
  something clearly heavier than what hit you (a bat, wrench, axe or sledgehammer against a crowbar or a machete; an
  axe or a sledgehammer against a katana). Broken off once, you can't be broken off again for 1.1 s, so a light
  weapon's spam (the katana's included) can't keep you from ever swinging back.
- **Ducking.** Crouch under a swing across (a sideways sweep) and it goes over your head. A chop from overhead still
  lands. Bots at the higher skills duck sweeps in Brawl, and parry more often.
- **Shoving a guard open.** A shove into someone's guard from in front knocks it aside without touching their stamina:
  the guard's down and can't come up for most of a second, and your next blow goes through any guard while it's open.
  Holding a guard against someone who shoves gets punished.
- **The katana's balanced.** It hits for less (28), swings a little slower, reaches a touch shorter, costs more stamina
  a swing and bleeds less often, so it's no longer the one weapon to spam.
- **Brawl kill perk.** In Brawl each kill gives you back half your health and half your stamina at once (bots too).
- **Boxes in one swing.** A melee swing knocks off every box on a shelf in its reach and arc on your side of the
  shelving (up to four), as a round does: on the Mall's stockroom shelving, Route 9's gondolas and the Farm's racks.

**Swings everyone sees.** Bots swing their melee weapons as you do: their arms, body and weapon go through a swing
recorded off a survivor swinging each weapon, frame by frame, the blow landing on its own timing (they used to only
twist their body). Other players' swings, in co-op and in the arena, and the bots' on a joiner's screen, are played
the same way the moment the swing's sent, rather than left to the pose sent a few times a second (which smeared a
third-of-a-second swing to nothing); the whoosh comes as the blow comes round.

**Hit reactions on your arms.** From your own eyes, a hit on you knocks the arms and whatever they're holding: shoved
back toward you and down, the muzzle thrown up and away from the side it came from, tipped over, shaking for a moment
as it settles back (less down the sights). A melee weapon is knocked the same way.

**Fairer fights with the bots** (all skills but the Terminator, which is meant to be relentless).

## Controls

These are the defaults. Every key except walking can be changed in **Settings → Controls**, either to another key or
to a middle or side mouse button. The keys shown in the game always match your choices.
Everywhere a key is named (the workbench's cards and notes, the gun and mod descriptions, the cards' feature lines)
it's the one you've set. Resetting the controls to their defaults asks first (a second click within a few seconds),
and resetting the other settings leaves the view you picked on the title alone. On the menus, Tab and Space work as on
any page (moving round the buttons and pressing them) and the arrows scroll; only while you play are they the game's.

F does two things: a press uses whatever's in front of you, a hold boards up a window. Keys you set before leaning came
in are kept, except that use, the bandage and the next kind of round move off E, Q and X if you'd left them there.

**Leaning.** Q and E lean you out to the left and right from the hips, at the hip or down the sights, round a corner or
over cover. Your upper body tips over (head, arms and gun with it, and everyone else sees you lean), your eyes go about
32 cm out to that side, and the view rolls a little with them, while your arms and the gun cant over further still
(about twice as far), so the gun lines up with the lean, down the sights too. It eases in and out over about a quarter of a second.
By default a press leans you and another press (or the other key) straightens you up; Settings, Camera, Lean switches
it to Hold, where you lean only while the key is held. You lean only as far as there's room, never through a wall. A
sprint, a vault or going down straightens you up, and leaning at the hip you walk a fifth slower (down the sights it
costs nothing more).

**How quickly the sights come up** depends on the gun and its weight: a handgun and the machine pistol are the
quickest (30% faster than the assault rifle), the revolver, the sub gun and the sawn-off next, then the assault rifle,
the shotguns, the battle rifle and the hunting rifle, and the machine gun slowest (a quarter slower). What's on it
slows it with its weight, as it slows your pace (its calibre, its magazine, every attachment), and a laser quickens
it a little (5% a tier).

**Crouching.** C crouches you: down on bent knees, the thighs forward and the hips about 28 cm lower, so your eyes go
from about 1.33 m to about 1.06 m and a crouched head is hidden behind waist-high cover that a standing one shows over.
It eases down and up (a little quicker going down). By default a press crouches you and another stands you up;
Settings, Camera, Crouch switches it to Hold. Crouched you walk at 55% of your pace with shorter steps, your
footsteps are about a third as loud (to you and to everyone else), and your spread is 35% tighter (from the hip and
down the sights). Crouched you're braced, too: the gun's sway and wander, from the hip and down the sights or a scope,
drop to 60% of standing, so the sight picture holds steadier, and every shot kicks 15% less (the view's climb, the
crosshair's walk and the gun's jump in your hands). From your own eyes the head goes down and comes up on a spring, sinking a touch past the crouch and
settling, the view nodding down as you drop and lifting as you rise (gently: under 2 degrees at its most, damped so
it barely carries past); the view is 3% narrower while crouched; and the gun and your arms cant in a little toward the
middle (a quarter as much down the sights) and lag a touch on the way down and up. A sprint or a vault stands you up; a vault from a crouch starts from it, the
hips coming up out of the bend into the vault's own crouch, never popping up to standing first. In the arena everyone
sees you crouch (your pose carries it), and a crouched player's head, chest and legs are all lower for the shots that
meet them; crouched behind low cover, nothing of you shows over it from above. Tear a board down moved to G, by the board
key F (a key you'd left on C, or on K, moves with it); G is also the underbarrel grenade, so at a boarded window or door
holding G tears a board down and never fires a grenade, and anywhere else it fires as before.

The fire mode by your ammo count only shows its B hint on a gun that can actually switch modes (the AR, or one
with a mod that adds a setting). Its little rounds are the gun's own cartridge: a 9mm's round nose, the 10mm's and a magnum's flat
nose, a rifle round's bottleneck and point, and a shotgun's red-hulled shell on its brass head. The double barrel

sway turns about your grip, so walking, strafing and turning never leave them lagging or jogging behind the view;
only their own small sway and bob move them.
**Vaults and landings from your own eyes.** Crouching for a vault you glance down at the sill or the rail, your hands
planted on it in view, each on its own side; the view pitches with your lean as you go over, rolls with the way you're
crossing and widens a touch at the top of the arc, then you land with a dip, a nod and a little rock that settle on a
spring (a tired scramble lands harder). With Vault fire most of it's held off, so you stay on the aim, and your gun's kept steady in the view the whole way over, carried by your eyes as it is on your feet (it used to go over with the vaulting body, swung up and round and trailing a moment behind where you looked). Your gun never leaves your hand for a vault: the right hand keeps hold of it (the left takes the sill), lowered out of the way unless you have Vault fire, and it comes back up on its own as you land, so it's never whipped off your back and back into your hands (a melee weapon is still put away and drawn after). Your eye keeps up with your body all the way over (it used to trail a hand's breadth behind it late in the jump). With Vault fire and your aim further round than your chest can twist, your head turns the rest on your neck, smoothly, and doesn't swing on past the aim as your hips come back under you. What rides on your back isn't drawn from your own eyes, so aiming high never fills the view with sleeves or a bat over your shoulder. The
station you get is the one you're looking at (a ray out from your eyes against each station's box, within about two
metres; nothing is picked up if you're not looking at one), outlined while you look at it, an open one staying open
while you're still in reach of it, and a click out on the game puts an open station away and takes the mouse
back, and coming off the sights doesn't replay the crosshair's last reload flash. From your own eyes the farm isn't an island in the haze: the woods go on past the road and past the trees
round the farm out to the horizon, thinner than the ring round the farm (about 790 trees out there), with broadleaf trees and taller pines among them, a power line runs along the far
side of the road (its wires swaying slowly in the wind, more as it blows harder and as a gust runs along them) and a
mailbox stands at the end of the drive, and a sky with drifting clouds and the sun's or moon's
glow sits over it all through the day and night. Looking toward the sun (or, far fainter, the moon) there's a lens flare off it: a soft glare
with a faint six-pointed star, and a few dim, faintly tinted ghosts of it strung across the view through its middle,
as a real lens throws them. It's only there while the sun or moon itself is in sight (no wall, roof or tree in front of
it), fades as it nears the edge of the view or the horizon, and thins in a fog round's fog.

**How the body moves.** Your survivor's animation runs through an animation graph, the way a game engine's does. Getting
about is a blend space: a forward stride, a backpedal and a side step each way are separate gaits, mixed by which way
you're going relative to where your hips face, so a diagonal is part stride and part side step, and the mix slides
round as you change direction. Running, each thigh's driven further up in front than it goes back behind, and the
standing knee gives under you mid-stance and straightens to push off; at a sprint the knees come right up through the
swing and the heels kick up under the seat, while the foot you stand on stays down under your hips. Walking backwards is its own walk: shorter steps, each foot lifted early and reached
back, landing toe first on bent knees. Strafing is a real side step: the leading foot steps out, the other closes in
after it, the hips riding over each foot and rocking as the other lifts, and the feet never cross. Over that, a state
machine knows whether you're getting about, landing, vaulting or getting up at the start of a run. Changing state
never snaps the pose: where each joint was, and how fast it was moving, is carried into the new state and dies away
smoothly over a moment (inertial blending). The same catches any joint that would jump from one frame to the next for
any other reason, so nothing pops.

**Right-handed.** Your survivor shoots right-handed: the right hand on the grip and trigger, working the hunting
rifle's bolt and the charging handle and thumbing its rounds in (to the cartridges on the right hip, up over the open
port, carried down onto it in the fingers, laid in on top of the rounds in the well and pressed down into it by the
thumb, then let go, the magazine's spring lifting it a hair up under the feed lips, the left hand holding the rifle
up by its fore-end), and the left on the handguard or the pump, fetching mags and feeding shells,
rounds and the revolver's speed loader. A speed loader dumps every round in the cylinder, spent or not, before the
fresh six go in (in 1.75 s, a touch quicker than it was; not said on its card). A shove in the middle of the
revolver's reload leaves the cylinder swung out through the shove, the reload going on after it, rather than snapping
it shut and swinging it out again. Between loading shells and working the pump, the bolt or the cylinder, the hands carry on from one
into the next without a jump: after the last shell the hand comes off the loading port, dips under the gun onto the
pump and takes hold of it before it's worked, and the hand on the bolt eases back to the grip. A pump or a bolt is
never yanked out of nowhere (the hunting rifle's bolt least of all: it waits a moment longer after the shot, about a
fifth of a second, for the kick to settle, the hand takes longer reaching for the knob, the gun rides the stroke on a
heavily damped spring with no wobble, and its stops knock the gun about half as hard and the view under half as much,
so the shot flows into the bolt rather than snapping to it): there's a beat first, the grip set and the gun easing a touch the other way from where the
stroke will throw it, then the stroke snaps through on the same timing as before (so the sound and the case flying out
still land on it), bounces off its stops and the gun follows through on its loose spring. Hold the trigger down after
a shot and the pump or bolt isn't worked till you let go of it (the gun stays on target, the action shut, for a
follow-up look), then it's racked; a pump with Slam fire is worked trigger down, as it fires as you pump it. A gun you've drawn doesn't
stop dead in the hold either: it carries on a hair up as it gets there and seats back into your hands, a heavy one
more. A draw is paced by how far the whole gun travels, its muzzle and butt as well as the grip, and it slows into its
sharp turns (a long gun's muzzle dipping into the low ready before it comes up to the shoulder), so nothing pops or
bounces on the way out of the holster or off the sling; from your own eyes a long gun's stock seats into your
shoulder as it arrives, a short jolt up and back that settles out smoothly over a fifth of a second. From your own
eyes the view goes a little with your hand as you swap: a glance down and a lean toward the hip as a sidearm's put
away, toward your back as a long gun goes over your shoulder, then the same way to wherever the next one's drawn from,
each a smooth swell of a degree or two and back. It's only the camera that moves; your aim stays where it was. From above, aiming down the sights brings the gun up on a spring just under critical, a few percent past and
settled in about a quarter second, and it drops back the same way when you let go. Loading the revolver a
round at a time, the cylinder's pushed shut by hand after the last one (or when you stop to shoot): the left hand goes
to the open cylinder, pushes it round into the frame till it clicks home, and goes back to the grip, the shot going
off once it's latched. A hand holding the gun is held to its place on it the way a constraint holds a bone in
Blender: the grip hand on the grip, the support hand under the handguard or round the pump, wherever the gun's held
and however far up you look, the arm reaching all the way to it rather than the hand stopping short and floating.
Taking a hand off (for a mag, a shell, the bolt) lets go of the constraint as the hand blends away, and taking hold
again blends it back on just as smoothly; a fresh mag rides in the fist wherever the fist really is (from your own eyes the hand takes it from just below the bottom of the view rather than down at the hip, and the long carry over to the gun is spread across half its way, so the hand isn't flung the last half metre in a few frames), and as its top
nears the magwell (a rifle's well, a pistol's grip) it's eased gradually onto the well's line in one smooth path,
never jumping into place, then pushed up it, never through the side of the gun and never past where it seats.

**From the mag to the charging handle.** A gun is only charged when it was run dry: reloaded with a round still in
the chamber (a tactical reload), every gun just swaps the mag, with no charging handle, no bolt worked on the
detachable-box rifle and no slide dropped on a pistol. Run dry, a pistol's slide is dropped one of two ways: slingshotted over the top by the support hand, or released with the slide stop, the support hand coming up off the grip so its thumb presses the stop down as the slide flies home. Run dry, on a rifle with a charging handle the right hand leaves the grip while the
left is still coming away from slapping the mag home, the two overlapping rather than one after the other. It eases
down off the grip a moment, then rises unhurried on a low arc to the handle, settles onto it, draws it back as the
spring takes up and lets it go; the gun stays canted in your hands through all of it instead of swinging back upright
in between, and the hand returns to the grip on a slower, easier path. From your own eyes the gun is let out a little
further, lower and turned onto its side for it, so the arm drawing the handle works low and to the side instead of
coming up across your view.

**The M4's flick.** With the AR's own 30-round box in it (any bigger mag reloads the usual way), a mag change is
flicked: the thumb hits the release, the support hand goes straight off the handguard for the pouch, and the trigger
hand eases the rifle a touch the other way, then snaps it over on the wrist, its top rolled in and the gun swung out to
the right, so the empty mag is flung out of the well mid-flick, at the snap's quickest, and goes tumbling off up and
out to your right (from your own eyes you see it go; on your teammates' screens too). The wrist whips through, carries
the rifle on past, rocks it back and holds it out a beat, then eases it slowly back into the reload while the support
hand is already coming up with the fresh mag, which goes straight up the well and is driven home hard enough to buck
the gun. Run dry, instead of the charging handle the heel of the support hand comes up off the mag and slaps the bolt
catch, the bolt slamming home. It's one continuous motion: the gun follows the wrist on a springy curve worked out
ahead, so nothing in it starts or stops dead and its speed never jumps, and the reload takes exactly as long as the
plain one.

**Back to the hold.** Once a reload's last beat is done, the gun comes out of the reload pose on a critically damped
spring: it sets off from rest, eases out and settles into the hold over about half a second, instead of leaving at
full speed the instant the mag is slapped home (the slap itself a softer bounce than before).

**Each gun handles like itself.** Every weapon has its own weight and give: a light pistol snaps through its
reload and its slide, a 9mm machine pistol is quick and crisp, the AR is balanced, the battle rifle and the auto
shotgun swing a little slower and carry on further, and the LMG lumbers into its reload and rocks back after. The
same slap home moves a light gun more than a heavy one. A mag change opens with a small wind-up of the wrist the other
way before the gun's rolled over to dump the old mag.

**Getting hit.** You always know where it came from: red floods in from that side of the screen (from the bottom
for a blow from behind) and holds a moment before it drains, a red arc round the middle of the view (round you, from
above) points at it and keeps pointing as you turn, fading over a second or so, and the view's knocked away from it and
shakes back along that line as it settles. In first person your head flinches from the blow: it snaps back from one in
front (forward from one behind), turns and tips away from the side it came from, the eye jolted back a couple of
centimetres, and comes straight back to where you were looking within half a second, harder for a harder hit and as
strong as **Screen shake** lets it. None of it covers the middle of the screen. Down the sights the flinch is a smaller jolt (under
half as big, with much less roll and none of the eye jolted back), and the view and the gun on your eye move as one,
so the sight picture stays together instead of the optic being knocked off your eye; the screen shakes about half as
much too.

**Low health.** Your hands tremble and the crosshair wanders, a heartbeat knocking it now and then: a slow, smoothed
tremble, small from your own eyes and kept calm from above too, never a jitter.

**Bandaging.** The gun goes away, your left forearm comes up across you and your right hand winds a roll of gauze
round it. The strip comes off the roll onto the arm turn by turn, just where the hand's gone round, the roll in your
fist thinning as it goes: one turn straight round to anchor it, then a spiral up the forearm with each turn over half
the last, a last turn straight round, and the end pressed down and taped. A short bandage gets fewer turns. The
forearm turns slowly in the hand as the gauze goes round, and the hand holding it drifts a little with your breathing,
never on a straight line: the arm comes up quickly and settles with a touch of overshoot, each turn of the roll
slows where it pulls the strip tight and quickens round the back, and the arm sways on a couple of slow, uneven waves. From your own eyes the view goes with the work rather than staring fixed: it tips down onto the arm and turns a touch toward it, tilted over to it, nods each time the strip's pulled tight over the top of a turn, sways after the hand as it goes round, rises and falls with a slow breath, and follows the wrap up the forearm as it goes on; all of it eased onto softly as the arms come up and settling back more slowly once the bandage is done.
In first person the forearm is held close in front of you, slanting across the middle of the view, and the view tips down a little
onto it, so the wrap goes on where you can watch it, and the right hand comes in round it with the roll (its shoulder
placed so the hand stays at the arm, the strip off the roll only a few centimetres long); it all stays where it is in the view as you look up or down, as the weapons do. Your teammates see your arms,
bandaging or with a melee weapon, turned where you're looking up or down, as they already did with a gun. Each bandage stays on your arm
till the wave's over (cleared, cards or no cards, they come off with the blood on you and your weapons), up to three of them up the forearm, wound snug over whatever's there: the bare arm, a sleeve, a
glove, the canvas jacket's sleeve, a rolled-up sleeve, a watch. Each one follows what's under it along the arm (tight on
a sleeve, up over a cuff's edge or a roll and straight back down past it, drawn up over a snap or a watch face the way
a bandage pulled tight is, never floating off the arm and never with anything poking through it), its corners pulled
tight round the arm's, and each turn sits only on the turns actually under it, a gauze layer's thickness over each, with a
spot of blood slowly seeping through each. The blood you pick up killing zombies up close stains your clothes and
skin, never a fresh bandage: those stay clean but for their own seep.

**Reloads that go wrong.** Hurt hands reload slower and shakier. They can fumble: the hands lose their rhythm,
slowing to about half speed and catching it back up (never stopping, which read as the game hitching), while the hand
with the mag juggles it, a dip, a catch and a wobble, the gun knocked partway through and something clacking. The revolver never fumbles: its rounds go in one at a time anyway. They can also miss the magwell, in one continuous motion: the fresh mag drifts off the well's line as it comes up,
knocks into its lip with a short clack (the gun jolted by it), bounces back off it as the bounce dies away, and is eased back onto the line and in. No two misses look alike: each is its own size (most small), its own bounce, and one after another come from alternate sides. That
can happen once, twice or up to three times running at low health, and once in a while with steady hands too. The mag
is only ever off the line below the well's mouth, so it never goes through the side of the gun. A hand leaving the gun
for a mag, a shell or a handle gathers speed rather than starting off at full tilt, and when a reload's done the hand
carries on back to its grip with the way it was going, settling onto it without stopping short or overshooting. Every one of these beats
is part of the reload's own length, so the reload ring fills over the whole reload as it really goes (a fumble or a
miss isn't known ahead: the ring fills as the reload's planned to go, and only stretches out once the hands actually
slip), and the old mag falling, the new one going home and their sounds all land on the animation's own marks. The
ring round the crosshair glides after the reload rather than stepping, and when a fumble or a miss sets it back it
slides back down with a flair: it flares red, the bead on its end swells and the ring gives a short shake. The shake
moves the ring on top of where it's drawn round the crosshair, so it stays in its place (it used to be thrown down
across the crosshair and stay there).

**A gun in each hand.** With the second pistol or second machine pistol fitted, left click fires the gun on your left and
right click the one on your right, and each gun kicks on its own shot from
your own eyes, flipping up on its wrist while the other stays steady, and running with them each hand pumps forward
and back with your stride, opposite the other, the guns canted out. Inspecting a pair (**Y**) has its own moves: each
gun is turned on its own wrist rather than the two swung about as one, the left a beat behind the right and mirroring
it. Both come up and in, roll onto their sides to show their outsides, roll right over to show their insides, stand
muzzles up and toed in so you see both muzzles side by side, then tip down so you look along the slides, and go back
onto the aim. From your own eyes they're brought up into view, held out where the fists don't hide them. A shot from
either gun (left or right click) ends it at once.

**Inspecting.** Press **Y** (it can be rebound) to look your gun over, from your own eyes or from above: it's brought
up and in, rolled over to show one side and then the other, swung round to see down its front, and tipped to look
along its back and top, your head following it. Each move starts gently, swings through and settles into its pose
with a little give, the hands never quite still. The hand on the grip keeps hold of it as it was however the gun's
turned (the wrist turns with it), and the gun's never held further out or nearer in than your arms comfortably reach,
so it never floats off the hand or turns in it. Standing close to a wall doesn't spoil it: the gun's no longer pushed
back and dipped away from the wall while you look it over (it's brought in close anyway), easing back into the wall's
push as you finish. A handgun's turned in the one hand, the other hanging at your side.
From your own eyes the gun's held up in front of you and the HUD and the crosshair are put away while you look (from
above they stay). Every sidearm but the revolver (the handgun and the .50 alike) is looked over the same way: brought
up close in the middle of your view and turned on its side in the hand, its muzzle tipped up to the left and its top
to the right, your thumb along the frame, then rolled right over to its other side, stood muzzle up and tipped down to
look along the slide. The revolver's twirled first: held out to the side on a loose wrist, a little wind back, then
flicked forward round your trigger finger for three turns, quick off the hand and slowing all the way into the palm
(never at a steady rate), caught with a small rock past the grip, and then looked over just like the handgun. With a
shoulder stock on it there's no twirling it: it's only looked over. Every long gun has its own way of being looked over, after what you'd check
on that one: the pump's rolled onto its side to look into the loading port, then
its ejection port, the bead and the ghost ring; the double's muzzles are tipped right up to look over the rib at both
bores before each side plate (the sawn-off, short and light, is handled on the wrist instead: tipped muzzles up close to the eye to look down both bores, rolled right over to each side plate, quicker, and tipped down along its stub of a rib); the auto shotgun shows its left side and its mag from below; the hunting rifle is
sighted along its top first, unhurried, then rolled to its bolt; the battle rifle's canted forty-five degrees each
way and lifted to look at its mag well; the machine pistol's light, so it's flicked about quickly; the SMG shows its
cocking tube, the front sight's hood and the drum sight; the AR its left side, the port and forward assist, and its
mag well; the LMG's heavy, so it's only hefted and tipped a little each way before a look over the feed cover. At the
end it's carried back onto the aim as smoothly as it was turned. Pausing or picking cards between waves doesn't cut it short or start it over: it comes to a stop right where it is under the menu, frozen mid-move from your own eyes and from above alike (the hands' small shake stops with it), and carries on from there once the menu's gone. Online (co-op or the arena with others), where the game never stops for your pause menu, it carries on playing under the menu as everything else does, and a click on the menu doesn't cut it short. A melee weapon's looked over its own way: a one-hander (the
crowbar, the wrench, the machete) is brought up in front of your face, turned to show one flat and then the other,
laid across to look along its edge and twirled once in the fingers before it drops back to the guard; the katana's
held across in both hands at the chest and turned, then stood up to look up its edge; the axe, the sledge and the bat
are hefted up in front of you, their head turned each way, then tipped out to your right and turned there. Each one's
carried from pose to pose on the same springs as its swings, slower for a heavy one. Firing or reloading puts the gun straight
back on the aim for it; aiming, sprinting, vaulting, switching weapons or pressing Y again eases it back.

## Settings

| Setting | Options |
| --- | --- |
| Ambient occlusion | On, off (soft shading in corners and creases; close to your eye, on the gun in your hands, it fades out rather than leaving a grainy pattern) |
| Glow | Off, soft, normal, strong |
| Shadow quality | High, medium, low |
| Resolution | Auto (lowers itself if the frame rate drops), 100%, 75%, 50% |
| Frame rate counter | On, off |
| HUD style | Normal, or Minimalist (the essentials only: no heart trace, rounds or ammo bar, just the count, no clock face and no picture of the melee weapon you're holding) |
| Theme colour | Ember, Crimson, Amber, Moss, Teal, Steel blue, Violet, Bone (the highlight on the menus, the HUD and the crosshair) |
| Colour blind mode | Off, protanopia (red), deuteranopia (green), tritanopia (blue): shifts the colours of the whole screen apart |
| Field of view | 60–100° (first person) |
| Mouse sensitivity | 25–200% (first person) |
| Mouse smoothing | Off (the view turns the moment the mouse does), on (eased over a few frames) (first person) |
| Master volume, music | 0–100% |
| Screen shake | 50–100% |

Frames are kept cheap where it doesn't show: the weapon lights' data reaches every lit surface through one small
texture rather than per-material lists that were sent again for each object drawn (over a thousand calls a frame), and
details too small to change a figure's outline or cast a shadow (an eye, a pin on a gun) are left out of the outline
pass.

All settings are saved in your browser.

## Working on the game

```bash
npm install
npm run dev        # builds and serves at http://localhost:5173, rebuilding on every save
npm run build      # minified build into dist/
npm run build:dev  # readable build with a source map, so errors point at src/js/<file>:<line>
npm run fix-imports # rewrites every file's imports and exports to match what it uses (after moving code about)
```

Every build also writes `dist/standalone.html`, the whole game in one file. Every push runs CI: all three builds and a
browser smoke test (`tools/smoke.mjs`) that starts a run, moves, shoots, reloads and pauses.

**Keeping a crowd cheap to draw.** A zombie you can see is about a dozen draws for its body and the same again for its
shadow, plus three more per body piece for the see-through outline that shows it behind cover from above. Those
see-through layers are only drawn when something could actually stand between the camera and the zombie: a march from
the zombie toward the camera across the farm's grid (walls, window frames, fences, props up to their height, trees'
crowns, the roof over the house, the woods past the edge) decides it, leaning the safe way. Its eyes, too small to
change its shape, get none. A gun rebuilt mid-run (an attachment, mod or upgrade bought at the bench) is merged back
into a mesh per material once its pieces settle, like one built at the start of a run, rather than staying a hundred
separate draws. With twelve zombies out in the yard at night that's about 930 draws a frame instead of about 1,290,
and with a weapon light and laser on the gun about 900 instead of about 1,470.
Every figure (you, the zombies, your teammates) also leaves out its pieces too small to see from where the camera is:
a piece under about a pixel across on screen (an eyelid, a buckle, a zip, a stowed gun's pin) is moved onto a layer
nothing draws, out of the frame, its shadow and its outline, and put back as the camera comes close enough for it to
show (first person, down the sights). Your pieces and your teammates' a little bigger than that, under four pixels,
stay in the frame but stop casting a shadow nobody could make out. Your own see-through outline (a fill, a silhouette
and a rim for each piece of you, some hundred and seventy draws) and your teammates' are only drawn when something
could stand between the camera and you, the same test as the zombies'. With 24 zombies round the house that's about
1,010 draws a frame instead of about 1,190, and in the yard about 1,060 instead of about 1,270.
Every gun also has a far version that looks the same but is made of far fewer triangles, drawn instead of the
detailed one once the whole gun is under about 150 pixels long on screen: the guns in the rack, on your back, in
your and your teammates' hands from above, a teammate's across the yard in first person. Each switches by its own size
on screen, for whichever camera is drawing it, with a little give either side so it doesn't flicker at the edge. The
gun in your hands in first person, or one you walk right up to, stays fully detailed. The far version keeps every
part's outline. Stocks are bent along the gun to within 0.8 mm instead of 0.1 mm. Barrels, rings and lathe-turned
parts get half the sides, and outlines keep only the points needed to stay within half a millimetre. Of the stippling and checkering, only the
faces you can see are kept (a dot's or a line's top, not its sides), so the grip texture still shows. A spitter's acid sacs are rounded with fewer faces once the spitter's under about 140 pixels tall.
In the house with the gun rack in view, that's about 211,000 triangles a frame instead of about 398,000.
The woods only draw the trees you can see. Each frame, trees out of view are left out of the frame, and from your
own eyes so are trees hidden behind nearer ones. That's checked against a small depth picture of the view: nearest
first, each tree that shows paints in boxes that sit inside its crown (a staircase up each tier of a pine, the middle
of a broadleaf's crown), and a tree whose whole outline is behind them is left out. The boxes never reach past the
boughs, so a tree you'd see through a gap is always drawn. Same-frame renders with and without it come out identical.
From your own eyes that takes the woods from 2,475 trees to about 300–650 drawn, depending on where you look. A tree
left out of the frame still casts its shadow, from a copy drawn only into the shadow map.
Everything else that stands still is culled the same way. From your own eyes (and the free camera) the house's outer
walls go into that depth picture first, as the faces of each run of wall cells. Window and door cells are left out, so
whatever you'd see through an opening is always drawn. So the woods behind the house are left out too. Then every
piece of the house, the furniture and the yard's props that's wholly behind a wall or a tree crown is left out of the
frame. From any view, a piece under about half a pixel across on screen (a hinge, a nail, a screw far off) is also left
out. Its size on screen comes from the camera's own field of view, so with a wide one small things far off go sooner,
as they look smaller. Pieces behind you or off to the side were already left out, against the same camera. Only the
frame is culled, never the shadow map (the sun sees what you can't) or a scope's view. Same-frame renders with and
without it came out identical. Facing the house from outside, a frame went from about 224,000 triangles and 600 draws to
137,000 and 390; from the side, 218,000 and 600 to 173,000 and 430; inside, about 15% fewer triangles.
The frame's own work was trimmed too. A profile of a wave in first person showed the game's code is a small share of
the frame and the drawing nearly all of it, so draws were what to cut. Past the culling above, the glow and the fog's
overlay passes no longer work out every object's place in the world over again, just after the frame itself did. Reading
where the muzzle or your eye is (asked for several times a frame) updates only the chain of parts it hangs from, not your
whole body; checked the same to the last digit. A piece of you or a teammate under 4 cm across casts no shadow, as the
sun's shadows are softened over more than that. That's 70 shadow draws of you a frame down to 62.
A kill used to stall the frame for 12 to 20 ms of the game's own code on its own, and it's what made the later waves,
with kills coming thick and fast, judder: the HUD replayed its little animations (the kill and scrap counters' pop,
the crosshair's hit marker, the streak and pickup notes, a damage number's bump on every hit) by asking the page where
an element was in between, which makes the browser lay the whole page out there and then, several times a kill. They're
replayed a frame later instead, without asking, and the counters' rolling digits the same way: a kill is now about
1.3 ms.
The trees outside the fence now cast shadows too. Those within reach of the sun's shadows cast real ones, and the far
woods (past about 32 m from the middle, beyond the shadow map) lay a soft shadow on the ground away from the sun,
as long as the tree's height over the sun's angle, turned with the sun through the day and fading with it. Every tree
outside the farm also has a soft pool of shade under it, as the farm's own do.
Every model was also put through a triangle pass that keeps every detail and only cuts triangles that don't need to be
there:
- **Stocks** (the hunting rifle's, the pump's, the double barrel's, the MP5's) are slimmed along the gun by cutting
  them into pieces. They used to be cut every 8 mm all over, so their big flat sides were tens of thousands of slivers.
  Now an edge is only cut where the slimming really bends it, until it's within a tenth of a millimetre of the curve.
  Flat stretches stay a few big triangles, and the wrist's curve gets as many as it needs.
- **Outlines:** every traced part drops the points it can do without (on a straight run, or more than a curve needs to
  stay within 0.04 mm), so its sides and faces have fewer triangles and an identical shape.
- **Stippling and checkering:** each dot keeps its flat top, with each side a single triangle down to the grip, and
  never its hidden face against the grip (6 triangles instead of 12). Each checkering line keeps its top and two
  sides, but not its hidden underside or its two ends, a millimetre square each (6 instead of 12). At a couple of
  pixels across they look the same.
- **Trees:** the trunk's hidden ends (one in the ground, one up inside the boughs) are gone. Every flat end is fanned
  from a corner, so an 8-sided cone's base is 6 triangles, not 8. The grass tufts' bases are fanned the same way. A
  tree gets lighter the less of it you can see. From your own eyes, one under 64 pixels tall, or with over 40% of it
  behind nearer trees, is drawn with 6-sided tiers on a 5-sided trunk. One under 26 pixels, or over 75% hidden, gets
  5-sided tiers on a 4-sided trunk. One wholly hidden isn't drawn at all. Same-frame renders with and without the
  lighter trees differ in about one pixel in a thousand. The trees' shadows are cast from that lighter version too,
  as the sun's shadows are softened over more than the two ever differ by.
- **The bat's** turned outline keeps only the points its curves need (a tenth of a millimetre), not 90 evenly spread.
- **Wrist pieces:** the beads, knots and chain links are only a few millimetres across, so they're rounded with fewer
  sides (a paracord band was about 23,000 triangles).
- **Tiny parts' shadows:** the house's, the furniture's and the props' shadows leave out parts under 3 cm, whose
  shadows the soft shadow edge would hide anyway.

**F9** opens the cheat menu, for testing on your own (never in co-op). Its search box looks through every cheat at

### How the code fits together

The game is plain JavaScript and three.js (r186, WebGL2), with no framework. The build bundles just the parts of
three.js the game uses into `vendor/three.min.js`. Each file in `src/js` is an ES module:

- A file imports what it uses from the other files and exports what they use from it, so the top of a file lists
  everything it depends on, and an editor can jump to where anything is defined.
- Another file's variable can only be read. The few that other files change come with a setter in their own file
  (`setShake`, `setZoom` and so on).
- The build checks every file's imports and exports (`tools/modules.mjs`). It fails, naming the file and line, on:
  - a name that isn't imported;
  - an import of something that isn't exported, or an import that isn't used;
  - an export no other file imports, or anything declared and never used;
  - an assignment to another file's variable;
  - a file using, as it loads, something from a file that loads after it.
- To move code from one file to another, move it and run `npm run fix-imports`: it rewrites the imports and exports
  of every file to match.
- For the browser, the build joins the files in the order of `src/js/load-order.json` into one script. That order only
  matters for code that runs at load time, such as building the world. The build also checks the joined script and
  names the file and line of any error, such as two files declaring the same name.
- Two pieces of one model whose faces lie in the same plane, facing the same way, flicker through each other where they
  overlap (z-fighting). The model helpers (`box` in `core/geometry.js`, `profile`, `solid` and `cyl` in
  `weapons/gun-parts.js`) note each face's plane and the patch it covers, per group; a face that would lie over one
  already there is pushed out a hair (0.1 mm on gun-sized parts, 0.6 mm on anything over 25 cm), so the later piece (the
  detail on a body, as models are built) always shows. Pieces side by side in a row aren't touched, and nothing is
  pushed more than three times. Build models body first, details after, and this takes care of itself.
- Work that needs nothing of the scene (painting a picture, walking a grid) can go to the game's worker threads:
  write it as one function that uses nothing outside itself (its source is all a worker gets), register it with
  `threadJob(name, fn)` from `core/threads.js`, and run it with `threadRun(name, data, transfer)`, which hands back a
  promise. It returns `{ out, transfer }`, and can draw on an OffscreenCanvas. Where there are no workers, the same
  function runs on the main thread.
- Each frame, `game/loop.js` runs `update(dt)` (`game/update.js`, the simulation) and then `visuals(dt)`
  (`game/visuals.js`, animation, camera and HUD). Those two files list every system in order, so they're the place to
  start reading.

```
src/
  index.html     the page: HUD, menus, settings, station panels
  styles/        CSS, one file per part of the screen
  js/
    core/        renderer, shaders, maths, geometry helpers, shadow batching
    world/       the farm: house, rooms, yard, props, collision, cut-away
    lighting/    light map, day and night, fog of war
    player/      movement, aiming, vaulting, bandages, gear
    weapons/     gun stats, models, mods, firing, reloading
    melee/       melee weapons and swings
    zombies/     types, spawning, pathfinding, damage, ragdolls
    barricades/  boards and grates
    stations/    workbench, sawhorse, cabinet, radio, gun rack, strongbox
    upgrades/    the cards and tiers
    effects/     post-processing (SSAO, bloom), blood, particles, outlines
    ui/          HUD, crosshair, settings, menus
    input/       keyboard, mouse, touch and key bindings
    audio/       sounds and reverb
    game/        shared state, the loop, the camera
    net/         co-op over WebRTC
tools/           build, smoke test, the asset packing scripts, and arena-plan.py (lays out and checks the arena)
assets/          the raw texture and sound packs (not shipped)
```

Each source file starts with a comment saying what it holds. The main shared state is `P` (the player), `W8` (your
weapons and ammo), `S` (your upgrade stats), `run` (the current run), `zombies`, and `state` and `phase` (which screen
is showing, and whether it's a wave or a break).

### Adding a gun

1. Add its stats to `GUN_WEAPONS` in `weapons/gun-stats.js`. Give it a rarity (`rar`) and a `desc`, and it will turn
   up as a card.
2. Add a model builder with the same id to `GUN_MODELS` in `weapons/gun-models.js`. Copying the closest gun is the
   easiest start.
3. Optionally add mods for it in `weapons/gun-mods.js`, a range drop-off in `weapons/ammo.js` and an ejection style in
   `weapons/casings.js`.

Melee weapons work the same way, in `melee/melee-stats.js` and `melee/melee-models.js`.
